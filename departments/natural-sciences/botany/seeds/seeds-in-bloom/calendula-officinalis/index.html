THE POT MARIGOLD

Calendula officinalis L.

Chapter I · The Living Plant

Botanical Form

The pot marigold is generally grown as an annual, though it may behave as a short-lived perennial or biennial in mild conditions. It belongs to the daisy family, Asteraceae. Its upright, branching stems are often lightly hairy, and its leaves are simple, green and arranged alternately along the stem.

What appears to be a single flower is actually a flower head made up of many small florets. Flower heads may be yellow, golden, orange, cream, or patterned with more than one shade. Plants can continue flowering over an extended period when conditions are favourable.

Calendula often self-sows: mature fruits fall near the parent plant and may produce seedlings in a later growing period.

Chapter II · Form and Identification

The Seed and Its Form

The structures commonly called calendula seeds are botanically dry, one-seeded fruits known as achenes. Their shapes can vary considerably, even within a single flower head.

  • Form: curved, hooked, boat-like, or almost ring-shaped.
  • Surface: often ridged, rough or uneven rather than smooth.
  • Colour: commonly grey-brown to brown as the fruits mature.
  • Variation: fruits from the outer and central parts of a flower head may differ in size and shape.

When examining a specimen, record the shape and surface of several fruits rather than relying on a single example. Variation is part of the identification record, not necessarily a defect.

Chapter III · The Cycle of Formation

From Flower to Fruit

  1. The flower head opens. Numerous florets form the apparent bloom; the outer florets are the ones most readily mistaken for petals.
  2. Pollination and fertilisation occur. Fertilised florets may begin developing fruits.
  3. The flower head fades. The colourful florets wither, while the developing fruits remain within the head.
  4. The fruits mature. Achenes become firmer and drier, and their characteristic shapes become easier to observe.
  5. The next generation begins. Mature fruits can be collected for sowing or left to fall and potentially self-sow.

Not every floret necessarily develops into a viable fruit. Record what is observed on the particular plant rather than assuming every flower head produces the same number.

Chapter IV · Renewal

Germination and Propagation

Calendula is generally straightforward to grow from seed and does not usually require cold stratification. It may be sown directly into prepared soil or started in containers.

  • Sow into a suitable, well-drained growing medium and cover lightly.
  • Keep the medium evenly moist, but not waterlogged.
  • Provide suitable light and temperature for the chosen cultivar and local conditions.
  • Observe seedlings as roots establish and the first true leaves develop.

Germination often occurs within about 5–15 days, though it can take longer—sometimes up to around 30 days—depending on seed quality and conditions. Removing spent flower heads may encourage continued flowering; leave selected heads in place if collecting seed is the goal.

Chapter VI · Preservation

Collection and Storage

  • Choose mature, dry flower heads and inspect the fruits before collection.
  • Allow collected fruits to finish drying in a dry, well-ventilated place if needed.
  • Store them in a labelled paper envelope, protected from heat, damp and direct sunlight.
  • Label the species, collection date, location and flower colour; add notes on plant health and growing conditions.
  • Keep deliberately collected seed lots separate from seedlings that appear through self-sowing.

A clear label makes a small collection useful for future comparison. If the source plant or identification is uncertain, note that uncertainty on the envelope.

Chapter VII · Field Notebook

Field Observation Notes

For each calendula specimen, record:

  • Flower colour and any variation across the plant.
  • Plant habit, height and branching.
  • Date of first bloom and approximate duration of flowering.
  • Fruit shape, colour and surface texture across several achenes.
  • Number of days to germination and the conditions used.
  • Approximate number of mature fruits per head, where countable.
  • Whether self-sown seedlings appeared nearby in a later period.
