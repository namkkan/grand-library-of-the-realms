Chapter I · The Living Plant

Botanical Description

Dahlia pinnata is a flowering perennial native to Mexico and a member of the daisy family, Asteraceae. It grows from underground tubers that store nutrients and allow the plant to regrow after its above-ground stems die back.

Its upright stems carry green leaves divided into pointed leaflets with toothed edges. Flower heads vary enormously in size, colour and form across cultivated dahlias: white, pink, red, purple and many other shades occur. In simpler flower forms, the central disc is visible; highly double varieties may have dense arrangements of florets that conceal it.

Dahlias generally flower from summer into autumn. They thrive in sunny positions with fertile, well-drained soil and require protection from hard frost, particularly where tubers remain in the ground.

Chapter II · Form and Identification

Seed Morphology

The structures commonly called dahlia seeds are technically achenes: dry, one-seeded fruits that do not open at maturity.

  • Shape: oblong and flattened, often narrow at one end and broader at the other, like a small paddle.
  • Colour: often dark brown to nearly black when mature.
  • Surface: usually fairly smooth, sometimes with fine longitudinal lines.
  • Size: some examples measure approximately 9–13 mm long, though dimensions vary by specimen.
  • Tip and base: a small crown-like remnant of floral structures may remain; a prominent feathery parachute is generally absent.

Fruit shape can vary even within a single plant. For an illustration plate, compare several specimens and show both narrow, elongated and broader, flattened forms.

Chapter III · The Cycle of Formation

From Flower to Seed

A dahlia flower head contains numerous individual florets, as in other members of the daisy family. In simpler flower forms, the central disc is visible and may produce fertile fruits. In many highly double ornamental varieties, floral structures are modified and seed production may be limited or absent.

After successful pollination and fertilisation, the ovary develops into an achene. As the flower head fades, the fruits mature and darken. The dried head can then be inspected for mature fruits, although not every flower will produce viable seed.

Seed or tuber? Plants grown from seed may develop new combinations of colours and flower forms. Dividing a healthy tuber, by contrast, preserves the characteristics of that individual plant when the division includes a viable growing point.

Chapter IV · Renewal

Germination and Propagation

Dahlias can be propagated by seed, by dividing tubers, or by taking cuttings. Each method serves a different purpose.

  • Sowing: sow seeds indoors in early to mid-spring, using seed compost in small pots or trays.
  • Temperature: warm conditions encourage germination; a heated propagator can be useful.
  • Moisture: keep the compost lightly moist, never saturated.
  • Germination: often around 1–3 weeks under favourable conditions.
  • Transplanting: move seedlings into individual pots once large enough to handle, then acclimatise them before planting outdoors after the last frost.
  • Tuber division: each division must have a viable growing point or eye.
  • Cuttings: young shoots can also be rooted to produce new plants.

Seed propagation produces variation. Tuber division is more reliable when the aim is to preserve a particular cultivated variety.

Chapter VI · Preservation

Seed Collection and Storage

  1. Choose a healthy plant and allow a flower head to remain until it has dried.
  2. Inspect the mature head for developed fruits; not all may be fertile.
  3. Remove the dry fruits carefully and separate them from loose floral material.
  4. Allow them to finish drying in a cool, well-ventilated place if needed.
  5. Store them in a labelled paper envelope, protected from heat and humidity.

Record the parent plant's flower colour and form. Compare seedlings with the original plant to observe how variable seed-grown dahlias can be.

Chapter VII · Field Notebook

Field Notes

Foliage

Record leaflet number, size, arrangement, toothed margins and the way leaves sit along the stem.

Flower structure

Compare single and double flower heads. Note whether the central disc is visible and whether the flower appears to develop mature fruits.

Fruits

Examine flattened, elongated achenes. Compare width, colour, surface markings and broader ends across several specimens.

Underground storage

Record the tuber cluster and its growing points. Unlike the fruits, tubers store resources that allow the plant to regrow after dormancy.
