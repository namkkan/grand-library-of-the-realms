How to Use the Archive

About These Study Sheets

This is not a conventional book. It is a growing reference archive made of standalone botanical study sheets, one for each documented tree species.

Each record gathers identification, seed structure, reproduction, dispersal, dormancy, germination, growth timelines, ecology, field observations and a reserved illustration plate. Details and time ranges belong to the named species and should not be assumed to apply to every tree.

Study Sheet 001 · Species Record

Wild Cherry

Prunus avium L. · Rosaceae

A deciduous flowering tree of temperate Europe and western Asia, studied here as the first example of a tree's journey from seed to reproductive maturity.

01 · Species Identification

Identity & Habitat

Common names: Wild cherry, sweet cherry, bird cherry.

Scientific name: Prunus avium L.

Family: Rosaceae.

Tree type: Deciduous broadleaf flowering tree.

Native range: Europe and parts of western Asia; widely cultivated and naturalised elsewhere.

Recognisable features: smooth reddish-brown bark with horizontal markings, toothed leaves and clusters of white spring flowers.

Habitat: Temperate woodland edges, clearings and sites with fertile, well-drained soil. It generally benefits from good light and adequate moisture and does not thrive in persistently waterlogged ground.

This record focuses on wild cherry, Prunus avium. Sour cherry (Prunus cerasus) and ornamental flowering cherries are distinct records if added later.
02 · Seed Anatomy & Morphology

Inside the Cherry Stone

The cherry is a drupe: a fleshy fruit whose inner fruit wall hardens into a stone around the seed.

  • Fruit: rounded and fleshy, often red to dark red or nearly black when ripe.
  • Stone: hard, woody endocarp that protects the seed.
  • Seed coat: a thinner covering surrounding the seed itself.
  • Embryo: the young plant contained within the seed.
  • Cotyledons: embryonic seed leaves that contain or receive reserves used during early development.
  • Identification: the hard stone enclosed by fleshy fruit is a key diagnostic feature.

The stone is generally oval to broadly ovoid, light brown to brown, hard and relatively smooth, sometimes with a subtle seam. Dimensions vary between trees and seed sources, so measurements should be recorded from the actual specimen rather than treated as a fixed species limit.

03 · Reproduction & Seed Formation

From Blossom to Fruit

  1. Flowering: clusters of white flowers generally open in spring. In parts of Europe this often occurs around April or May.
  2. Pollination: insects, including bees, transfer pollen between flowers. Wild cherry is generally self-incompatible, so compatible pollen from another tree is commonly needed for reliable fertilisation.
  3. Fertilisation: following successful pollination, the ovules develop into seeds.
  4. Fruit development: the fruit's fleshy layers develop around the hard stone.
  5. Ripening: fruits often mature in early summer, though local climate and weather shift the timing.

Flowering does not guarantee a crop. Frost, poor pollination, pests or unsuitable weather can interrupt fruit development.

04 · Fruits, Cones & Dispersal

How the Seed Travels

Structure: fleshy drupe, not a cone.

Birds and mammals eat the fruit and may carry or excrete the stones away from the parent tree. Gravity also moves fallen fruits over short distances. This dispersal can bring seeds to new sites where seedlings may establish.

The hard stone protects the seed during dispersal but also forms part of the barrier that makes germination more complex than simply placing a fresh stone in warm soil.

05 · Wild Cherry Seed Classification

How the Cherry Seed Is Classified

Wild cherry is a flowering broadleaf tree in the rose family, Rosaceae. Its seed develops inside a fleshy drupe: the fruit's inner wall hardens into the stone that surrounds the seed.

  • Plant group: angiosperm (flowering plant).
  • Tree group: deciduous broadleaf.
  • Fruit type: drupe.
  • Seed position: enclosed within the hard stone inside the fleshy fruit.
  • Primary dispersal: animals and gravity.

These features identify wild cherry specifically and distinguish its fruit from the winged fruits of maples, the nuts of oaks and the cones of many conifers.

06 · Dormancy, Viability & Germination

A Seed That Must Wait

Wild-cherry seeds exhibit dormancy. A freshly collected stone may not germinate promptly even when moisture and warmth are available.

  • Dormancy: often requires seasonal conditioning; one or more seasonal phases may be involved.
  • Cold stratification: moist chilling is commonly used to help break dormancy.
  • Warm–cold treatment: some seed lots respond to a warm phase followed by a cold phase.
  • Viability: depends on maturity, handling, moisture and storage conditions.
  • Storage: wild cherry is described as an orthodox seed in forestry guidance, meaning suitably dried seed can be stored under controlled cool or freezing conditions. Exact protocols matter.
Dormancy is not a fixed timer. Seed source and treatment can produce different results, so a single duration should not be presented as universal.
07 · Preparing Seeds for Germination

Nursery Preparation

  1. Collect fully mature fruits and remove the fleshy pulp.
  2. Clean the stones carefully and discard visibly damaged material.
  3. Apply an appropriate moist stratification treatment for the seed source.
  4. Sow after the required conditioning, using a suitable, well-drained medium.
  5. Maintain moisture without waterlogging and monitor for emergence.

Forestry sources describe cold stratification and warm–cold sequences for wild cherry. The correct approach depends on seed lot and local practice; this record does not prescribe one treatment as suitable for every source.

Seed-grown trees are genetically variable. Grafting is commonly used to preserve the characteristics of named fruit cultivars.

Handling note: cherry stones and seeds are propagation material, not food. Cherry seeds contain cyanogenic compounds; do not crush or consume them.

08 · The First Stages of Growth

From Radicle to Seedling

  • Radicle emergence: the embryonic root emerges from the seed.
  • Shoot emergence: the young shoot grows towards and eventually above the soil surface.
  • First leaves: the seedling begins expanding its photosynthetic surface.
  • Root establishment: roots develop to absorb water and nutrients.
  • Juvenile growth: the young tree gradually builds its stem, branches and canopy.

Successful establishment depends on moisture, light, soil aeration and freedom from excessive competition. Drought, frost and browsing animals can reduce survival.

09 · From Sapling to Mature Tree

Growing into the Canopy

Wild cherry can grow relatively quickly in favourable conditions. Mature trees commonly reach around 15–25 metres, although the eventual height depends on the site and growing conditions. A European genetic-conservation guide gives a typical lifespan of approximately 70–100 years.

  • Growth rate: relatively rapid when light, moisture and soil conditions are favourable.
  • First flowering: some trees may flower within a few years, but age varies considerably with origin and growing conditions.
  • First viable seeds: may follow the first successful flowering and pollination, but the first flowers do not guarantee a crop or viable seeds.
  • First substantial harvest: often occurs later than the first flowering and may take a decade or longer in some seed-grown trees; this is a broad indication, not a guaranteed age.
  • Mature structure: full canopy and trunk development take much longer than the beginning of reproduction.
Age-at-flowering and age-at-seed-production figures are especially sensitive to whether a source describes wild forest trees, nursery seedlings or grafted cultivars. These categories must not be conflated.
10 · Flowering, Fruiting & First Harvest

When Does a Cherry Tree Bear Fruit?

Flowering generally precedes fruit maturation by several weeks. In temperate European conditions, blossoms often appear in spring and cherries ripen in early summer, with substantial local variation.

A seed-grown wild cherry may begin flowering after several years in favourable conditions, but a reliable first harvest is not guaranteed at the same age. Pollination compatibility, weather, tree health and the genetic identity of the tree all affect fruit production.

Grafted orchard trees can fruit earlier than seed-grown trees because the grafted scion comes from mature reproductive material. The exact age depends on cultivar, rootstock and cultivation.

11 · Tree Lifecycles & Environmental Factors

The Year of a Wild Cherry

Spring · Flowering

White flowers open and insect pollinators visit. Following successful fertilisation, fruits begin to develop.

Early summer · Fruiting

Cherries ripen and are eaten by wildlife, which can disperse the stones.

Autumn · Senescence

Leaves change colour and fall as the deciduous tree enters its dormant season.

Winter · Dormancy

Growth slows or stops. Seasonal cold also forms part of the tree's annual cycle and is relevant to the conditioning of its seeds.

Exact dates shift with latitude, elevation, weather and cultivar. Soil drainage, water availability, light and competition also influence growth and reproductive success.

12 · Wild Cherry Ecological & Cultural Record

Life Beyond the Wild Cherry

Ecological role: the flowers provide a seasonal resource for pollinating insects, while the fruits feed birds and mammals. Dispersed stones may establish new trees away from the parent.

Human uses: cultivated sweet cherries are eaten fresh and used in cooking. Wild-cherry wood is valued for woodworking and furniture, and the tree is planted ornamentally for spring blossom and autumn colour.

Cultural note: cherry blossom is associated with spring and seasonal renewal in several traditions. Particular customs should be documented by region rather than presented as universal.

Study Sheet 002 · Species Record

The Apple Tree

Malus domestica Borkh. · Rosaceae

A deciduous fruit tree cultivated across temperate regions, with several seeds developing inside the central core of its accessory fruit.

01 · Species Identification

Identity & Habitat

Common names: Apple, domestic apple.

Scientific name: Malus domestica Borkh.

Family: Rosaceae.

Growth form: Deciduous fruit tree.

Habitat and cultivation: Widely cultivated in temperate climates. Orchard trees grow best where the cultivar and rootstock suit the local climate, soil and available water.

Recognisable features: a spreading crown, grey-brown bark that becomes more fissured with age, toothed leaves and spring clusters of flowers, often white with pink-tinged buds.

This record concerns the domestic apple, Malus domestica. Crab apples and ornamental species of Malus can have different traits and should receive separate records when included.
02 · Seed Morphology

Seeds Inside the Apple Core

The apple is an accessory fruit classified as a pome, not a drupe. Several seeds may develop inside the central core, each enclosed by its own seed coat.

  • Form: ovoid to teardrop-shaped.
  • Colour: brown to dark brown when mature.
  • Surface: smooth, firm and sometimes slightly glossy.
  • Size: small, usually several millimetres long; size varies.
  • Structure: seed coat enclosing the embryo and stored reserves.
  • Number: an apple may contain several seeds; the number varies with cultivar, pollination and fruit development.

Seeds that appear fully formed are not automatically viable. The pale tissues of the core are not the seeds themselves, just as the whole apple is not a seed.

03 · Reproduction & Seed Formation

From Blossom to Apple

  1. Flowering: apple trees generally bloom in spring. Exact dates depend on cultivar, climate and local weather.
  2. Pollination: bees and other insects carry pollen between flowers. Many cultivars need compatible pollen from another cultivar flowering at the same time for reliable crops.
  3. Fertilisation: successful pollination allows seeds to develop inside the flower's ovary; seed development also influences normal fruit development.
  4. Fruit growth: the apple enlarges over several months as the flesh develops and the seeds mature inside the core.
  5. Ripening: colour, flavour, texture and storage qualities vary greatly by cultivar.

Poor pollination can mean fewer developed seeds and may increase the chance that young fruit drops. Flowering alone does not guarantee a harvest.

04 · Fruit & Seed Dispersal

How Apple Seeds Travel

Animals may eat apples and transport the seeds away from the parent tree. Fallen fruit can also leave seeds near the tree, while humans have spread apple trees across continents through cultivation, trade and selection.

Unlike wild cherry, whose seed is enclosed in one hard stone, the apple contains several separate seeds within its central core. Cultivated apples are often harvested and transported before natural dispersal occurs.

05 · Seed Classification

How the Apple Seed Is Classified

  • Plant group: angiosperm (flowering plant).
  • Tree group: deciduous broadleaf fruit tree.
  • Fruit type: pome, an accessory fruit.
  • Seed position: several seeds lie within the central core.
  • Primary dispersal: animals and human cultivation.

The key distinction from the wild cherry is the fruit structure: apple seeds are individually enclosed in the core of a pome, whereas the cherry seed lies inside a hard stone within a drupe.

06 · Dormancy, Viability & Germination

Breaking Apple Seed Dormancy

Mature apple seeds commonly exhibit physiological dormancy. In nature, moist winter cold helps release dormancy before germination in a favourable season.

  • Common treatment: clean seeds from mature fruit and keep them moist during cold stratification.
  • Indicative duration: around two to four months is a useful general reference, not a universal rule.
  • Germination: the seed absorbs water, the embryo resumes growth and the primary root emerges, followed by the shoot and first leaves.
  • Viability: appearance alone cannot confirm whether a seed will germinate.
Stratification needs can vary between seed lots and conditions. Germination may be uneven, and some seeds may not germinate.
07 · Preparing Apple Seeds for Sowing

Propagation Notes

  1. Collect seeds from mature apples and remove the surrounding pulp.
  2. Clean the seeds and record the cultivar or source tree if known.
  3. Place seeds in a suitable moist medium for cold stratification, avoiding waterlogging.
  4. Check periodically for mould and early root emergence, handling germinated seeds carefully.
  5. Sow in a well-drained growing medium and maintain appropriate moisture and light.

Seed propagation is valuable for botanical study and breeding, but it is not a reliable way to reproduce a named apple cultivar true to type.

08 · Germination & Seedling Growth

From Root to Young Apple Tree

  • Seed stage: the embryo remains inside the seed until dormancy has been sufficiently relieved and conditions allow growth.
  • Germination: the primary root emerges first and begins anchoring the plant and absorbing water and minerals.
  • Seedling: the shoot rises and develops its first leaves; the plant gradually becomes less dependent on stored seed reserves.
  • Juvenile tree: roots, trunk and crown develop over successive growing seasons.
  • Establishment: young seedlings are vulnerable to drying, unsuitable temperatures, competition and physical damage.
09 · Growth & Reproductive Maturity

From Seedling to Fruit-Bearing Tree

An apple tree grown from seed can remain juvenile for several years. Under favourable conditions, first flowering may occur at roughly six to twelve years, sometimes later; individual trees vary considerably.

Grafted nursery trees can fruit much sooner because the grafted portion comes from mature reproductive material. Their timing depends on cultivar, rootstock and growing conditions.

Seed-grown trees are genetically variable. The fruit of a seedling may differ from the apple that supplied the seed in size, colour, flavour, texture and harvest time.

10 · Flowering, Fruiting & First Harvest

First Blossom Is Not First Harvest

Apple trees usually flower in spring, and fruit develops over several months. Fruit development from flowering to maturity is often around 120–150 days, but cultivar and environmental conditions can shift this considerably.

A seed-grown tree may take many years to produce its first apples, and the first crop may be small. A substantial harvest can take a decade or longer in some trees. Grafted orchard trees often begin fruiting around two to five years after planting, depending on cultivar and rootstock.

These are reference ranges, not deadlines. Flowering does not guarantee fruit set: compatible pollination, weather, tree health and fruit development all affect the result.

11 · The Apple Tree Through the Seasons

A Year in the Orchard

Spring · Blossom

Flower clusters open and insects carry pollen between compatible flowers.

Late spring and summer · Fruit development

Successfully pollinated flowers develop into young apples; seeds mature inside the core as the fruit grows.

Late summer to autumn · Ripening

Harvest time varies by cultivar and climate. Fruit colour, texture and flavour change as apples mature.

Autumn and winter · Dormancy

Growth slows as the tree enters its dormant period. Moist winter cold also helps release dormancy in apple seeds.

The exact seasonal sequence depends on region, weather, cultivar and orchard management.

13 · Ecological & Human Record

Beyond the Orchard

Ecological role: apple blossoms provide a seasonal resource for pollinating insects. Fallen fruit and apples consumed by animals can contribute to seed dispersal.

Human uses: apples are eaten fresh and used in cooking, juice and other foods. Long-term cultivation has produced a wide diversity of cultivars selected for flavour, storage, appearance and growing conditions.

Botanical importance: seedlings show genetic variation, making seed-grown apples useful in breeding and study even when they do not preserve a cultivar's traits.

Study Sheet 003 · Species Record

The Lemon Tree

Citrus × limon (L.) Osbeck · Rutaceae

An evergreen citrus tree whose fragrant flowers and acidic fruits may develop in several flowering and fruiting cycles under suitable conditions.

01 · Species Identification

Identity & Habitat

Common names: Lemon, lemon tree.

Scientific name: Citrus × limon (L.) Osbeck.

Family: Rutaceae.

Growth form: Evergreen fruit-bearing tree.

Climate: Warm, sunny environments sheltered from severe frost. Growth and flowering depend strongly on local temperature and cultivation conditions.

Recognisable features: upright, spreading branches that may bear sharp thorns; alternate, elongated, leathery leaves with finely toothed margins; fragrant flowers, usually white inside and sometimes purplish outside.

Unlike the wild cherry and domestic apple, the lemon tree is evergreen in suitable conditions. It may produce new growth and flowers in several flushes rather than following one strictly defined annual season.
02 · Seed Morphology

Seeds Inside the Citrus Fruit

The lemon is a citrus fruit known as a hesperidium, a specialised type of berry with a leathery outer rind and internal segments.

  • Form: small, oval or teardrop-shaped.
  • Colour: pale cream.
  • Surface: generally smooth.
  • Number: varies by fruit and cultivar; some lemons contain few seeds, others several.
  • Structure: a seed coat enclosing an embryo capable of developing into a new plant.

Collect seeds from fully developed, healthy fruit. Record their condition before sowing: immature, damaged or poorly stored seeds may fail to germinate.

03 · Flower to Seed

Flowering, Pollination & Fruit Formation

The reproductive cycle begins with flower buds opening into fragrant blossoms. Depending on variety and growing conditions, a lemon tree may flower repeatedly through the year.

Pollination occurs when pollen reaches a receptive stigma. Outdoor insects commonly assist. Many lemon cultivars can set fruit using their own pollen, although reproductive behaviour varies among varieties.

After successful fertilisation, the ovary develops into a young lemon. The fruit gradually enlarges, the rind thickens and the internal segments develop around the seeds.

Not every flower becomes a lemon. Flowers may fall without setting fruit, and young fruit may drop before maturity. These losses are a normal part of the reproductive cycle.

The period from flowering to harvest may range from approximately four to twelve months, depending on cultivar, temperature, water availability and growing conditions. Full size does not necessarily mean the fruit has reached final colour or maturity.

04 · Dormancy & Germination

Germinating a Fresh Lemon Seed

Lemon seeds do not generally require the prolonged cold stratification associated with some temperate tree species. Under warm, moist conditions, they can germinate relatively quickly.

  1. Remove the seeds from a healthy, fully developed lemon.
  2. Clean away adhering pulp.
  3. Sow in a suitable, well-drained growing medium.
  4. Keep the medium moist without waterlogging it, and provide warmth.
  5. Record the sowing date, seed condition, temperature, moisture and date of emergence.

Seeds intended for immediate sowing should not be allowed to dry excessively, since moisture loss and storage conditions can affect citrus seed viability.

Under favourable conditions, seedlings may emerge in approximately two to six weeks, although germination can take longer.

The radicle (embryonic root) emerges first, anchoring the seedling and beginning water and mineral uptake. The shoot then grows upward and produces the first leaves.

05 · Developmental Stages

From Seedling to Mature Tree

  1. Germination and emergence: the embryo resumes growth and produces a root and shoot. At this stage, the seedling is vulnerable to waterlogging and abrupt temperature changes.
  2. Early seedling: the first leaves expand and the root system branches. Light, warmth and steady moisture support establishment.
  3. Juvenile tree: the trunk thickens, branches develop and the crown begins to take shape. Thorn formation may be prominent in young growth or particular cultivars.
  4. Mature vegetative growth: the tree develops a larger canopy and root system and may produce repeated flushes of new shoots and leaves.
  5. Reproductive maturity: the tree begins to flower and set fruit. The age at which this occurs depends strongly on propagation method and growing conditions.

Seed-grown trees may remain juvenile for many years. Grafted nursery trees often produce fruit considerably sooner. Record first flowering separately from the first successful harvest.

07 · Propagation & Variation

Growing True to Type?

Growing a lemon tree from seed is useful for observing the full developmental cycle from embryo to mature plant. It is not the most reliable method for reproducing a particular cultivated lemon.

Seedlings may differ from the tree that produced the fruit. Some citrus varieties can form embryos through processes that preserve maternal characteristics, while others show greater variation. The outcome depends on the cultivar and its reproductive biology; not every seed-grown lemon will be identical to its parent.

Cultivated lemon trees are therefore commonly propagated by grafting or budding onto selected rootstocks. Cuttings may also be used in appropriate circumstances.

Always record the propagation method. Without it, observations about growth rate, flowering age and fruit quality may be misleading.

Reference Section

Glossary, Index & Botanical Records

Drupe: a fleshy fruit with a hard inner layer surrounding its seed.

Endocarp: the innermost layer of a fruit wall; in cherries it forms the stone.

Stratification: a controlled moist-temperature treatment used to overcome dormancy in some seeds.

Viability: the capacity of a seed to remain alive and capable of germination under suitable conditions.

Radicle: the embryonic root that emerges during germination.

Hesperidium: a citrus fruit with a leathery rind and segmented, juice-bearing interior.

Evergreen: retaining foliage throughout the year under suitable conditions.

This glossary and index will expand as new tree species are added to the study archive.
