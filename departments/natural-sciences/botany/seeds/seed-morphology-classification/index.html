About This Work

Purpose and Scope

This volume introduces the external form and identifying features of common food seeds and grains. It is intended as a practical reference for students of botany, field researchers, gardeners, seed collectors and members of the Library’s botanical departments.

The first group brings together three cereals and three legumes. These familiar food plants provide a useful comparison of grain-like fruits and true seeds, as well as differences in outline, surface, markings and visible points of attachment.

In strict botanical usage, the grains of cereals are caryopses: dry, one-seeded fruits in which the fruit wall is fused to the seed coat. The everyday word “seed” is retained in this guide where it makes the entries easier to use, while important distinctions are noted in the relevant records.[2]

Preliminary Pages

A Note from the Author

A seed is easily overlooked. It is often small, frequently mistaken for a mere fragment of the fruit that carried it, and rarely examined with the patience it deserves.

Yet its shape, surface, markings, and internal structure may provide valuable clues to its identity. Such clues must be observed carefully; resemblance alone is not proof, and a convenient classification is not necessarily a correct one.

This volume has been prepared as a practical introduction to the recognition and description of common seeds. It concerns familiar plants: those cultivated for food, gathered from gardens, encountered along roadsides, or collected beneath ordinary trees.

No specimen is too commonplace to merit accurate observation.

The reader is advised to measure before comparing, record before concluding, and preserve uncertainty wherever the evidence remains incomplete.

Professor Elowen Thorne

Food Seeds · Record 01

Wheat

Triticum aestivum
Group: Cereal · Family Poaceae · Botanical unit: caryopsis[2]

The wheat grain is small and elongated, commonly golden to light brown. A conspicuous longitudinal crease runs along one side and is one of its most useful external recognition features.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record the length, width and relative proportions; note whether the ends are narrow or rounded.
  • Longitudinal crease: observe its depth, continuity and position along the grain.
  • Surface and colour: record colour under neutral light and note any hairs, dullness or surface variation.
  • Orientation: compare the creased side with the opposite, more convex side.

Diagnostic Note

Wheat, like other cereal grains, is botanically a caryopsis rather than a naked seed: the fruit wall is closely fused to the seed coat.[2] Do not assume that colour alone is sufficient for identification; varieties and processing can alter appearance.

Record standard: measure an intact, unprocessed grain before comparing it with milled or polished material.

Food Seeds · Record 02

Rice

Oryza sativa
Group: Cereal · Family Poaceae · Botanical unit: caryopsis

Rice grains are typically narrow and elongated, though their proportions vary among cultivars. This entry is particularly useful because rice may be examined with the husk still attached or after dehulling, allowing the observer to distinguish the covering structures from the grain itself.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: compare length and breadth; record whether the grain is slender or relatively broad.
  • Husk present: note the colour, texture and shape of the outer floral coverings.
  • Dehulled grain: examine the exposed surface and record whether bran layers remain or have been removed during further processing.
  • Ends and edges: observe tapering, rounding and any visible damage from handling.

Diagnostic Note

The husk is not the seed coat. In rice, the persistent outer coverings are floral structures; after dehulling, the caryopsis is exposed. Record the preparation state whenever measurements or photographs are compared.

Comparison rule: do not compare a husked grain directly with polished white rice as though both show the same external structures.

Food Seeds · Record 03

Maize

Zea mays
Group: Cereal · Family Poaceae · Botanical unit: caryopsis[3]

The maize kernel is larger than the grains of wheat and rice in this introductory set. Its outline may be rounded, angular, broad or relatively narrow, depending on the type of maize and the position of the kernel on the ear.

Features to Examine

  • Overall shape: record whether the kernel is rounded, wedge-like, broad or elongated.
  • Profile: compare the broad face with the side view; note the thickness and curvature.
  • Surface and colour: document the colour and any mottling or surface damage without treating colour as a species-level identifier.
  • Attachment end: inspect the basal area where the kernel was attached to the cob, if preserved.

Diagnostic Note

As in wheat, the maize grain is a caryopsis. The fruit wall adheres closely to the seed coat, and the kernel contains an embryo and a substantial storage tissue called the endosperm.[2][3]

Comparison rule: shape varies widely among maize types; describe the specimen before assigning it to a particular variety.

Food Seeds · Record 04

Lentil

Lens culinaris
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed

The lentil is a small, flattened seed with a broadly disc-shaped or lens-like outline.[1] Its shape makes it a useful contrast with the elongated cereal grains and the more irregular forms of the bean and chickpea.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: observe the degree of circularity and whether the margin is even or slightly angular.
  • Thickness: compare the face view with the edge view to document how strongly the seed is flattened.
  • Colour: record the seed coat colour and any mottling; colours vary among cultivated types.
  • Surface and hilum: examine the surface closely and locate the small hilum, the scar marking the point of attachment.

Diagnostic Note

Do not use colour as the sole distinguishing feature. A consistent record of outline, thickness, margin and hilum is more useful when comparing lentils from different cultivars or sources.

Comparison rule: photograph or draw the seed both face-on and edge-on; one view alone can conceal its characteristic flattening.

Food Seeds · Record 05

Common Bean

Phaseolus vulgaris
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed[4]

The common bean often has a kidney-shaped outline and a relatively smooth seed coat. Seed colour and pattern vary considerably across cultivated forms, so the entry should focus on structural features rather than on a single expected appearance.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: note the curve of the inner and outer margins and the degree of kidney-like indentation.
  • Hilum: locate the hilum, the scar left where the seed was attached within the pod; record its length, position and colour.
  • Seed coat: describe whether the surface is smooth, glossy, dull, mottled or speckled.
  • Variation: record the seed's colour and pattern, but compare these alongside shape and hilum features.

Diagnostic Note

The hilum is a particularly useful landmark when describing bean seeds. Use the same orientation in drawings and photographs so that records can be compared consistently.

Terminology: the correct term is hilum, not “hilium”. It identifies the attachment scar, not a separate organ.

Food Seeds · Record 06

Chickpea

Cicer arietinum
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed

The chickpea is rounded but irregular in outline, often with a visibly rough or uneven surface and a small beak-like prominence. These features distinguish its general form from the flattened lentil and the kidney-shaped common bean.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record the uneven rounded shape and whether the seed is compact or more angular.
  • Surface: describe the degree of roughness, wrinkling and any shallow depressions.
  • Prominence: locate the small beak-like projection and record its direction and prominence.
  • Colour and hilum: document the seed coat colour and identify the hilum where visible.

Diagnostic Note

The chickpea's surface and projecting feature should be described independently. Do not confuse natural surface irregularity with damage caused by drying, storage or handling.

Comparison rule: compare a whole, undamaged specimen with other legumes before drawing conclusions from a broken or heavily abraded seed.

References & Identification Notes

The descriptions in this introductory group are based on standard botanical terminology and general morphological features. Cultivar, processing and specimen condition can affect visible characteristics; measurements should therefore be taken from the specimen in hand.

Editorial Principle

This is a working field guide, not a substitute for examination of the specimen. The entries distinguish observed morphology from interpretation and leave room for later measurements, illustrations and revised notes. Future records may expand this group with additional food seeds and comparative specimens.
