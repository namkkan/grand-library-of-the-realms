Wheat

Triticum aestivum
Group: Cereal · Family Poaceae · Botanical unit: caryopsis[2]

The wheat grain is small and elongated, commonly golden to light brown. A conspicuous longitudinal crease runs along one side and is one of its most useful external recognition features.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record the length, width and relative proportions; note whether the ends are narrow or rounded.
  • Longitudinal crease: observe its depth, continuity and position along the grain.
  • Surface and colour: record colour under neutral light and note any hairs, dullness or surface variation.
  • Orientation: compare the creased side with the opposite, more convex side.

Diagnostic Note

Wheat, like other cereal grains, is botanically a caryopsis rather than a naked seed: the fruit wall is closely fused to the seed coat.[2] Do not assume that colour alone is sufficient for identification; varieties and processing can alter appearance.

Record standard: measure an intact, unprocessed grain before comparing it with milled or polished material.

Rice

Oryza sativa
Group: Cereal · Family Poaceae · Botanical unit: caryopsis

Rice grains are typically narrow and elongated, though their proportions vary among cultivars. This entry is particularly useful because rice may be examined with the husk still attached or after dehulling, allowing the observer to distinguish the covering structures from the grain itself.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: compare length and breadth; record whether the grain is slender or relatively broad.
  • Husk present: note the colour, texture and shape of the outer floral coverings.
  • Dehulled grain: examine the exposed surface and record whether bran layers remain or have been removed during further processing.
  • Ends and edges: observe tapering, rounding and any visible damage from handling.

Diagnostic Note

The husk is not the seed coat. In rice, the persistent outer coverings are floral structures; after dehulling, the caryopsis is exposed. Record the preparation state whenever measurements or photographs are compared.

Comparison rule: do not compare a husked grain directly with polished white rice as though both show the same external structures.

Food Seeds · Record 03

Maize

Zea mays
Group: Cereal · Family Poaceae · Botanical unit: caryopsis[3]

The maize kernel is larger than the grains of wheat and rice in this introductory set. Its outline may be rounded, angular, broad or relatively narrow, depending on the type of maize and the position of the kernel on the ear.

Features to Examine

  • Overall shape: record whether the kernel is rounded, wedge-like, broad or elongated.
  • Profile: compare the broad face with the side view; note the thickness and curvature.
  • Surface and colour: document the colour and any mottling or surface damage without treating colour as a species-level identifier.
  • Attachment end: inspect the basal area where the kernel was attached to the cob, if preserved.

Diagnostic Note

As in wheat, the maize grain is a caryopsis. The fruit wall adheres closely to the seed coat, and the kernel contains an embryo and a substantial storage tissue called the endosperm.[2][3]

Comparison rule: shape varies widely among maize types; describe the specimen before assigning it to a particular variety.

Food Seeds · Record 04

Lentil

Lens culinaris
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed

The lentil is a small, flattened seed with a broadly disc-shaped or lens-like outline.[1] Its shape makes it a useful contrast with the elongated cereal grains and the more irregular forms of the bean and chickpea.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: observe the degree of circularity and whether the margin is even or slightly angular.
  • Thickness: compare the face view with the edge view to document how strongly the seed is flattened.
  • Colour: record the seed coat colour and any mottling; colours vary among cultivated types.
  • Surface and hilum: examine the surface closely and locate the small hilum, the scar marking the point of attachment.

Diagnostic Note

Do not use colour as the sole distinguishing feature. A consistent record of outline, thickness, margin and hilum is more useful when comparing lentils from different cultivars or sources.

Comparison rule: photograph or draw the seed both face-on and edge-on; one view alone can conceal its characteristic flattening.

Common Bean

Phaseolus vulgaris
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed[4]

The common bean often has a kidney-shaped outline and a relatively smooth seed coat. Seed colour and pattern vary considerably across cultivated forms, so the entry should focus on structural features rather than on a single expected appearance.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: note the curve of the inner and outer margins and the degree of kidney-like indentation.
  • Hilum: locate the hilum, the scar left where the seed was attached within the pod; record its length, position and colour.
  • Seed coat: describe whether the surface is smooth, glossy, dull, mottled or speckled.
  • Variation: record the seed's colour and pattern, but compare these alongside shape and hilum features.

Diagnostic Note

The hilum is a particularly useful landmark when describing bean seeds. Use the same orientation in drawings and photographs so that records can be compared consistently.

Terminology: the correct term is hilum, not “hilium”. It identifies the attachment scar, not a separate organ.

Chickpea

Cicer arietinum
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed

The chickpea is rounded but irregular in outline, often with a visibly rough or uneven surface and a small beak-like prominence. These features distinguish its general form from the flattened lentil and the kidney-shaped common bean.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record the uneven rounded shape and whether the seed is compact or more angular.
  • Surface: describe the degree of roughness, wrinkling and any shallow depressions.
  • Prominence: locate the small beak-like projection and record its direction and prominence.
  • Colour and hilum: document the seed coat colour and identify the hilum where visible.

Diagnostic Note

The chickpea's surface and projecting feature should be described independently. Do not confuse natural surface irregularity with damage caused by drying, storage or handling.

Comparison rule: compare a whole, undamaged specimen with other legumes before drawing conclusions from a broken or heavily abraded seed.

Pumpkin

Cucurbita pepo
Group: Cucurbit · Family Cucurbitaceae · Botanical unit: seed

The pumpkin seed is typically oval, flattened and pale cream, with a distinct margin around its outline. Shape and size vary among cultivated forms.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record the oval shape and degree of asymmetry.
  • Margin: observe the raised or clearly defined edge around the seed.
  • Surface: note smoothness, colour and any subtle surface markings.

Diagnostic Note

A broad, flattened profile and conspicuous margin are useful general features, but size and colour alone cannot identify a cultivar.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Cucumber

Cucumis sativus
Group: Cucurbit · Family Cucurbitaceae · Botanical unit: seed

Cucumber seeds are elongated and flattened, usually pale ivory to cream, with ends that may taper gently. Mature seeds are generally smooth.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: compare length and width and note whether the ends are pointed or rounded.
  • Surface: record colour, smoothness and any visible marks.
  • Profile: observe the thin, flattened form from the side.

Diagnostic Note

Compare mature, fully developed seeds; immature seeds may be thinner and less fully coloured.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Eggplant

Solanum melongena
Group: Nightshade · Family Solanaceae · Botanical unit: seed

Eggplant seeds are small, flattened and commonly cream to light brown. Their compact oval outline can be difficult to distinguish without magnification.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record the oval or rounded outline and relative flatness.
  • Colour: describe the range from pale cream to light brown.
  • Surface: use magnification to note fine texture and the margin.

Diagnostic Note

Colour varies with maturity and cultivar, so use several features rather than colour alone.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Tomato

Solanum lycopersicum
Group: Nightshade · Family Solanaceae · Botanical unit: seed

Tomato seeds are small and flattened, often oval or teardrop-shaped. Their surface may appear finely hairy or fuzzy under magnification.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: note the asymmetrical oval shape and any tapering.
  • Surface: inspect the fine hairs or fuzzy texture under magnification.
  • Colour: record pale straw to light tan tones in a cleaned, dry specimen.

Diagnostic Note

The apparent fuzz is a surface feature; avoid describing dried pulp residue as part of the seed coat.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Bell Pepper

Capsicum annuum
Group: Nightshade · Family Solanaceae · Botanical unit: seed

Pepper seeds are generally flat, thin and rounded to disc-shaped, with a pale yellow or ivory colour.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: compare roundness and note any slight irregularity.
  • Profile: record the thin, disc-like form.
  • Surface: observe the pale colour and smooth or finely textured surface.

Diagnostic Note

Seed size and outline vary among pepper varieties; record the specimen rather than assuming a fixed diameter.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Garden Pea

Pisum sativum
Group: Legume · Family Fabaceae · Botanical unit: seed

Garden pea seeds are commonly rounded and firm, although some varieties have wrinkled rather than smooth mature seeds.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record whether the seed is spherical, slightly angular or irregular.
  • Surface: distinguish a smooth coat from a wrinkled one.
  • Hilum: locate the attachment scar where it can be seen.

Diagnostic Note

Wrinkled and smooth forms both occur in cultivated peas; surface texture should not be treated as a universal species-level distinction.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Carrot

Daucus carota
Group: Carrot family · Family Apiaceae · Botanical unit: dry fruit

What is commonly called a carrot seed is botanically a small dry fruit. It is elongated, brownish and ribbed, and may retain fine bristles.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: note the narrow, elongated form.
  • Ribs: inspect the longitudinal ridges with magnification.
  • Surface: record colour and whether fine bristles remain.

Diagnostic Note

The planted unit is usually a dry fruit, not an isolated seed. Record this distinction in the specimen label.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Lettuce

Lactuca sativa
Group: Daisy family · Family Asteraceae · Botanical unit: dry fruit (cypsela)

Lettuce planting units are narrow, elongated and pointed, often with a fine beak. Their colour varies between cultivars and may range from pale to dark tones.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: measure the narrow body and pointed ends.
  • Beak: observe whether a slender terminal point is present.
  • Colour: record the tone of the dry fruit without using it as the sole identifier.

Diagnostic Note

The familiar planting unit is a dry fruit, commonly called a seed in horticulture.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Radish

Raphanus sativus
Group: Mustard family · Family Brassicaceae · Botanical unit: seed

Radish seeds are small and rounded, often reddish brown to brown, with a firm outer coat.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: note the rounded shape and any slight angularity.
  • Colour: describe the seed coat under consistent light.
  • Surface: check for a smooth, firm coat or small irregularities.

Diagnostic Note

Colour may vary between seed lots; examine several specimens before recording a typical appearance.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Garden & Vegetable Seeds · Record 10

Spinach

Spinacia oleracea
Group: Amaranth family · Family Amaranthaceae · Botanical unit: fruit

Spinach planting units may be angular, rough or spiny, depending on the cultivar. The unit commonly handled as a seed is a fruit containing the true seed.

Features to Examine

  • Outline: record angular projections and overall shape.
  • Surface: distinguish roughness from pronounced spines.
  • Variation: compare several units from the same seed lot.

Diagnostic Note

Spinach cultivars differ in fruit form. State whether the specimen is smooth, angular or spiny instead of assuming one universal shape.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

Garden & Vegetable Seeds · Record 11

Beetroot

Beta vulgaris
Group: Amaranth family · Family Amaranthaceae · Botanical unit: fruit cluster (seedball)

The unit sold or planted as a beet seed is commonly a rough, corky fruit cluster, often called a seedball, which may contain more than one true seed.

Features to Examine

  • Form: describe the irregular, knobbly outline.
  • Surface: note the corky texture and ridges.
  • Structure: distinguish the planted fruit cluster from the true seeds it contains.

Diagnostic Note

A beet seedball may produce more than one seedling because it can contain multiple true seeds.

Observation rule: describe the specimen in hand and separate visible features from assumptions about its variety or growing conditions.

References & Identification Notes

The descriptions in these introductory groups are based on standard botanical terminology and general morphological features. The garden and vegetable section distinguishes true seeds from dry fruits or fruit clusters commonly used as planting units. Cultivar, processing and specimen condition can affect visible characteristics; measurements should therefore be taken from the specimen in hand.

Editorial Principle

This is a working field guide, not a substitute for examination of the specimen. The entries distinguish observed morphology from interpretation and leave room for later measurements, illustrations and revised notes. Future records may expand these groups with ornamental flower seeds, further garden specimens and comparative records.
