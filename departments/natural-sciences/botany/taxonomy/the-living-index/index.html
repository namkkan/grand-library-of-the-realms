About This Work

The Living Index is a systematic guide to the identification, naming and classification of plant life throughout the Known Realms. It introduces the principles used by botanical scholars to describe species, compare specimens, organise collections and record the extraordinary diversity of the world's flora.

This volume establishes the methods and terminology used throughout the botanical collections of the Grand Library. It also considers the difficulties posed by hybrid plants, unfamiliar ecosystems and species whose properties do not fit comfortably within established classifications.

Preface

From the author's preface to the third revised edition.

There are few errors in natural philosophy more persistent than the belief that a thing, once named, has been understood.

A botanist encounters a flower growing between the stones of a ruined observatory. She records its height, the arrangement of its leaves, the number of petals, the colour of its pollen, and the character of its roots. She compares these observations with existing descriptions, determines its nearest relatives, assigns it a place within the accepted hierarchy, and enters its name into the appropriate register. Her work is careful. Her conclusions are justified. Her discovery is worthy of publication.

And yet, when she closes her notebook, the flower remains almost entirely unknown.

She may understand how it grows, but not why it blooms only beneath a cloudless night sky. She may know the structure of its roots, but not why those roots avoid the graves of certain kings. She may have established, beyond reasonable dispute, that it belongs to a particular family, while remaining unable to explain the faint sound it produces when winter approaches.

This is not a failure of taxonomy. It is a reminder of its proper limits.

The purpose of classification is not to exhaust the mysteries of the living world, but to establish a reliable means by which those mysteries may be studied. We classify so that observations may be compared, discoveries communicated, errors corrected, and knowledge accumulated beyond the limits of a single lifetime. A name allows one scholar to speak meaningfully to another, even when they have never stood upon the same soil or breathed the same air.

Without such a system, the botanist of the southern marshes might describe a plant unknown to the northern mountains, unaware that another scholar had already documented the same species beneath a different name. A physician might prepare a remedy from a familiar herb, while an unfamiliar regional variety, bearing a similar appearance, possesses entirely different properties. A collector might catalogue a rare flowering vine as an ornamental curiosity, failing to recognise that its disappearance would threaten the survival of several creatures dependent upon it.

Classification does not prevent every such mistake. It makes their discovery, discussion, and eventual correction possible.

This volume has been prepared in the belief that the flora of the Known Realms deserves a system of study equal to its extraordinary diversity. From the grasses that survive upon wind-scoured plateaus to the immense trees whose crowns obscure the oldest forest roads; from the salt-tolerant reeds of the western coasts to the pale subterranean growths found beneath abandoned cities, plant life occupies environments of remarkable variety. Some species are familiar to generations of rural communities, while others have been encountered only once, recorded in a traveller’s journal, and never reliably observed again.

Between these extremes lies a vast body of knowledge: incomplete, unevenly distributed, and too often inaccessible beyond the regions in which it was first gathered.

The Grand Library possesses collections assembled over centuries by explorers, gardeners, physicians, natural philosophers, agriculturalists, archivists, and scholars whose names have survived only in the margins of their notebooks. Its herbaria preserve specimens gathered from distant provinces. Its archives contain descriptions written in languages no longer spoken. Its botanical gardens maintain living examples of species that have vanished from their native habitats. Its catalogues preserve both the conclusions of celebrated authorities and the corrections of those who followed them.

These collections are invaluable. They are not, however, infallible.

An old specimen may have been incorrectly identified. A respected naturalist may have mistaken a seasonal form for a separate species. A plant recorded as extinct may continue to grow in a valley inaccessible for half the year. A local name may refer to several unrelated organisms, while a single species may possess a dozen names across different territories. Such difficulties are not peculiar to botanical study. They are the ordinary consequences of attempting to describe a living world through incomplete observations and imperfect records.

The scholar must therefore approach inherited knowledge with both gratitude and suspicion.

No authority, however distinguished, should be exempt from examination. No classification should be preserved merely because it is old. No observation should be dismissed solely because it contradicts an established description. Where the evidence is uncertain, uncertainty must be recorded. Where specimens disagree, the disagreement must be investigated. Where a species cannot yet be placed with confidence, it is preferable to leave its position unresolved than to manufacture certainty for the sake of a tidy catalogue.

A well-maintained index should make ignorance visible as readily as knowledge.

The present edition has been revised to reflect new collections, corrected identifications, disputed classifications, and several discoveries that have required the reconsideration of long-standing assumptions. Particular attention has been given to the relationship between preserved specimens and living plants, to the documentation of variation within species, and to the study of organisms whose characteristics do not conform neatly to the categories established by earlier botanists.

The latter subject requires a word of caution.

Across the Realms, some plants exhibit properties that remain poorly understood. Certain species respond to forms of magical influence; others produce unusual light, alter their growth in response to nearby organisms, or display behaviours that challenge conventional distinctions between growth, movement, and response. These phenomena deserve careful investigation. They do not justify abandoning the ordinary principles of botanical observation.

A luminous leaf is still a leaf, provided its structure warrants the term. A root that grows against gravity must be described as accurately as one that follows it. A plant altered by magical exposure should be compared with unaffected specimens wherever possible. The unfamiliar is not made more intelligible by removing it from systematic study, nor does an unexplained property excuse the observer from recording what can be established.

Magic is one influence among the many that may shape the life of a plant. It is not a universal explanation for every phenomenon that escapes our understanding.

Indeed, some of the most remarkable botanical discoveries in the history of the Grand Library have proved, upon closer examination, to require no extraordinary explanation at all. Unusual colours have arisen from ordinary variations in pigment. Apparent nocturnal movement has been traced to changes in temperature and moisture. A reputedly prophetic flowering season has been found to coincide with the migration of a particular insect. These corrections have not diminished the wonders concerned. They have revealed them more clearly.

I have spent thirty-two years studying, collecting, comparing, and occasionally misidentifying the flora of the Known Realms. I have returned to sites where I once believed I had completed my work, only to discover that I had overlooked an entire population. I have rejected conclusions that once seemed certain, and defended others long after the evidence had begun to turn against them. More than once, a student has drawn my attention to something I had failed to notice.

I consider these experiences among the most valuable parts of my education.

The botanist who cannot admit error will eventually cease to learn. The botanist who assumes that every question must have an immediate answer will mistake haste for progress. And the botanist who sees the natural world merely as a collection of specimens to be named will overlook the relationships that make each specimen meaningful.

A plant does not exist independently of its surroundings. Its form is shaped by soil, water, climate, competition, disease, and countless other conditions. Its survival may depend upon an insect, a migrating animal, a particular pattern of seasonal rainfall, or the continued existence of a neighbouring species. To understand a plant is to begin studying the web of circumstances in which it lives. Classification provides the framework for that study; it cannot replace it.

For this reason, The Living Index should not be read as a final catalogue of the world’s vegetation. No such catalogue can yet be written, and I doubt that one will ever be completed in any permanent sense. New species will be found. Old descriptions will be corrected. Populations will change, habitats will disappear, and organisms once thought impossible will be encountered by observers sufficiently fortunate—or sufficiently attentive—to recognise them.

This book is instead intended as a common foundation: a guide to the principles, methods, and records through which botanical knowledge may be organised and advanced.

The chapters that follow address the identification of plants, the construction of taxonomic groups, the use of scientific names, the examination of morphological characters, and the preservation of reference specimens. They consider the difficulties posed by variation, hybridisation, unfamiliar environments, and anomalous growth. They also examine the responsibilities of those who collect and describe the living world, particularly when their work may influence the preservation of rare species or the communities that depend upon them.

Wherever possible, I have distinguished established observations from hypotheses, and hypotheses from conjecture. Readers will find references to disputed cases throughout the text. These have been retained deliberately. A scientific record that conceals disagreement may appear orderly, but it is less useful than one that makes the boundaries of current knowledge plain.

I ask only that the reader bring to this work three habits of mind: patience in observation, precision in description, and humility in conclusion.

Look closely before deciding what you see. Record what you observe rather than what you expected to find. Compare before assigning a name. Preserve the evidence upon which your conclusions depend. And when the world presents something that does not fit your understanding, resist the temptation to force it into a familiar shape.

The purpose of an index is to help us find what we seek.

The purpose of a living index is to remind us that there will always be more to find.

1. The Need to Classify Plant Life

A botanist returning from an expedition rarely brings home a complete account of what she has seen. More often, she returns with a collection of specimens, a field journal full of hurried observations, several questions that could not be answered on site, and the uncomfortable suspicion that the most interesting plant was the one she failed to collect.

The work begins in earnest after the journey.

Leaves are examined beside earlier collections. Measurements are checked against the notes. Drawings are compared with specimens preserved in the herbarium. Names found in old regional catalogues are traced through successive editions, sometimes leading to a description that appears to match the plant, sometimes to an entirely different organism. What seemed straightforward in the field can occupy weeks of study.

This is the ordinary business of botanical classification. It is less dramatic than discovering an unknown forest, but without it, much of what is discovered would remain little more than an anecdote.

1.1. A common language for the study of plants

The first difficulty in studying plants is not always recognising them. It is establishing that different people are talking about the same organism.

In the villages along the western coast, a low-growing plant with thick, silvery leaves is commonly called sea’s ear. Inland, the same name is used for a small fungus that grows on fallen branches. In one province, widow’s herb refers to a plant traditionally placed upon graves; in another, the name belongs to an unrelated species used to dye mourning cloth.

These names are not necessarily incorrect. They belong to local traditions and often carry information that a formal scientific name does not. A village name may tell us when a plant flowers, which animals feed upon it, or how it has been used for generations. The difficulty arises when a name is treated as though it identifies one species everywhere.

For this reason, botanists require a system of naming that can be used across regions and languages. A scholar examining a specimen in the northern archives must be able to compare it with a description written by someone who collected it on the opposite side of the Realms. The two researchers may use different languages and have no knowledge of each other’s customs, yet their observations must remain comparable.

Scientific names provide this shared reference. They do not replace local names, nor should they be used to dismiss the knowledge of those who have lived among a plant for generations. Rather, they allow that knowledge to be recorded alongside observations made elsewhere, provided the identity of the organism has been established with sufficient confidence.

A name is useful only when its meaning can be traced to a particular organism or taxonomic group. The name alone is not proof of identity.

I once examined a collection of dried leaves sent to the Grand Library by a provincial physician. Every packet bore the same name, and the accompanying letter assured us that the contents had been gathered from a single species. On examination, however, the collection proved to contain three distinct plants. Two were closely related; the third belonged to a different family altogether. All three had been gathered under the same local name because their leaves possessed a similar bitterness.

The physician had not been careless. He had recorded the plants according to the knowledge available to him. The error arose because the name was assumed to be more precise than it was.

The lesson is a simple one: botanical communication must rest upon identifiable evidence, not merely upon agreement over words.

1.2. Recognition, identification and classification

These three activities are closely connected, but they are not interchangeable.

Recognition is the first impression that a plant resembles something already known. An experienced gardener may recognise a familiar group of plants from the shape of its leaves or the manner in which its stems grow. Such judgement can be remarkably reliable, but it remains an initial assessment.

Identification is the process of determining which known plant a particular specimen represents. This requires comparison with descriptions, identification keys, illustrations and reference specimens. A botanist may recognise that a plant belongs to a familiar group while remaining unable to distinguish it from two or three closely related species.

Classification places identified organisms into a wider system of groups. It asks not only what a plant is called, but which other plants it should be grouped with and which characteristics justify that placement.

Consider three shrubs collected from different elevations along the same mountain range. All possess narrow leaves and small white flowers. At first glance, they might appear to be varieties of a single species. Closer examination reveals differences in the arrangement of their flowers, the structure of their fruits and the form of their seeds. Further evidence may show that two are closely related, while the third belongs to a separate lineage that happens to grow under similar conditions.

The initial resemblance was real. The conclusion drawn from it was premature.

Plants can look alike without being close relatives. Conversely, members of the same species may differ considerably when they grow in different environments. Sunlight, moisture, soil conditions, age and seasonal changes can all affect a plant’s appearance. The taxonomist must learn which differences are useful for distinguishing species and which reflect ordinary variation.

This is one reason why identification should not be based upon a single character whenever several can be examined. The shape of a leaf may be informative, but its value increases when considered alongside the flowers, fruits, stems, growth habit and other available evidence.

In some groups, the decisive differences are so small that they cannot be seen without magnification. In others, the reproductive structures are known only from a handful of specimens. There are also plants that cannot be identified confidently when collected outside their flowering season. In such cases, a responsible botanist records the uncertainty rather than assigning a name simply to complete the catalogue.

The distinction matters. An incorrect identification may be repeated in subsequent books, copied into medicinal records and accepted by researchers who have never examined the original specimen. Once this happens, a single mistake can become difficult to separate from the accumulated body of knowledge.

1.3. Why plants are grouped together

A list of individual species would be of limited use if every plant had to be studied independently. Classification allows botanists to recognise patterns and apply knowledge gained from one organism to the investigation of others.

The simplest groups are often practical ones. Trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and aquatic plants are familiar categories based largely upon growth form and habitat. They are useful when describing vegetation, planning a collection or finding a plant in the field. A gardener searching for climbing species, for example, does not need to begin by consulting every flowering plant in the catalogue.

These categories have limits. A plant may grow as a shrub in an exposed mountain valley and become a small tree in a sheltered forest. Some species change their growth form with age, while others cannot be placed neatly into a single category. Practical groupings help organise observations, but they do not necessarily represent the deeper relationships between plants.

Formal botanical classification attempts to arrange organisms according to a broader understanding of their similarities, differences and relationships. The resulting hierarchy allows a species to be considered at several levels: alongside its closest relatives, within its genus, within its family, and among larger groups of plants.

The value of this arrangement becomes apparent when a new specimen is discovered.

Suppose an expedition returns with a flowering plant whose fruit is unknown to the Grand Library. Its leaves, stem structure and reproductive organs resemble those of a well-studied genus. If that placement is supported by sufficient evidence, the botanist gains a useful starting point. Existing descriptions can guide further examination, and the known characteristics of related plants can suggest which features deserve particular attention.

This does not mean that the new species must behave exactly like its relatives. A relationship is not a guarantee of identical properties. It is a framework for comparison.

The distinction is particularly important when a plant is being considered for medicinal, agricultural or conservation purposes. Similarity may provide a reason to investigate a possible use, but it cannot establish that the use is safe or effective. Those conclusions require evidence of their own.

Classification narrows the field of inquiry. It does not remove the need for inquiry.

1.4. Classification as a record of relationships

For much of botanical history, scholars grouped plants according to the characters that were easiest to observe. The shape of a leaf, the colour of a flower or the number of stamens could provide a convenient means of separating one group from another. Such systems were often practical and sometimes remarkably successful, particularly when the plants concerned were well known.

Yet visible resemblance can be misleading.

Two unrelated plants growing in dry, exposed country may develop similar small leaves because both must limit water loss. Plants that live in darkness may lose pigmentation. Those growing in marshes may develop comparable structures for surviving in saturated soil. The same environmental pressures can produce similar features in organisms that are not especially close relatives.

The reverse is equally troublesome. Closely related plants may develop very different forms as they adapt to contrasting habitats.

A classification based entirely upon outward appearance can therefore group unrelated species together or separate species that share a more recent common ancestry.

Modern botanical study draws upon a wider range of evidence. Morphology remains essential, but it may be considered alongside anatomy, development, reproductive biology, ecological information and, where suitable material is available, molecular evidence. No single source of information is sufficient for every group. The value lies in determining whether independent lines of evidence support the same conclusion.

The records held by the Grand Library illustrate this problem particularly well. Among the older collections are several plants historically grouped together because their flowers possess five prominent lobes. Later examinations revealed considerable differences in their internal structures and reproductive development. In some cases, the original group was divided; in others, apparently distinct groups were brought together after closer study.

Such revisions can be inconvenient. Labels must be amended, catalogue entries cross-referenced, and older publications interpreted according to the classifications used at the time. Researchers may disagree about the boundaries of a group even when they agree upon most of the evidence.

Nevertheless, a classification must remain open to correction.

The purpose of grouping plants is not to preserve the arrangement that is easiest to remember. It is to provide an increasingly useful account of the relationships supported by the available evidence.

1.5. The practical consequences of a name

Taxonomy is sometimes regarded as an academic pursuit concerned chiefly with terminology. This view is difficult to maintain when one considers the consequences of confusing two species.

In medicine, the identity of a plant may determine whether a preparation is useful, ineffective or dangerous. A remedy described in an old text may refer to a species that no longer grows in the region where the text is being consulted. A substitute may have acquired the same common name without possessing the same chemical properties. Before conclusions can be drawn about its use, the plants must be identified separately.

Agriculture presents similar difficulties. A crop’s wild relatives may contain useful traits, including resistance to local diseases or tolerance of poor soils. Identifying these relatives accurately can help researchers determine which populations deserve further study. An incorrectly identified specimen may send months of work in the wrong direction.

Conservation depends upon reliable identification as well. A survey cannot establish the distribution of a rare species if the plants recorded under its name include several unrelated organisms. Nor can the disappearance of a population be confirmed simply because a familiar plant has not been seen in its expected location. The original identification, the survey methods and the condition of the habitat must all be considered.

Within the Grand Library, classification has an additional responsibility. Collections must be organised so that specimens can be found, compared and examined by future researchers. A preserved plant without reliable documentation is of limited value. Its appearance may still be interesting, but the circumstances of its collection may be impossible to recover.

For this reason, the identification of a specimen should be accompanied by records of its origin, collection date, habitat, collector and the person responsible for its identification. Where uncertainty remains, that uncertainty should be made explicit. Later scholars may possess better reference material or methods unavailable to the original collector.

The specimen must be allowed to outlive the conclusion drawn from it.

A label may be corrected. A catalogue may be reorganised. The preserved evidence should remain available for examination whenever its condition permits.

1.6. What classification cannot tell us

There is a temptation, particularly among inexperienced naturalists, to treat a classification as though it were a complete description of the organism. Once a plant has been placed in its proper family and genus, they assume that its important characteristics have been established.

The assumption is understandable. A well-constructed classification gives order to an otherwise bewildering diversity. It allows unfamiliar plants to be approached through familiar groups and provides a sense that the subject is becoming manageable.

But a place in the hierarchy is not a complete account of a living thing.

A taxonomic description may tell us how a plant differs from its relatives. It may establish the structure of its reproductive organs, the range of its known distribution and the conditions under which it has been collected. It cannot, by itself, explain every aspect of its ecology, the full range of its chemical properties or the way its population will respond to a changing environment.

Nor should the absence of an explanation be taken as evidence that a plant possesses extraordinary properties.

The archives contain numerous reports of plants believed to move towards particular individuals, bloom in anticipation of distant events or change their appearance in the presence of certain emotions. Some reports remain unresolved. Others have been traced to ordinary environmental responses, misidentification or errors in observation. A few concern organisms whose behaviour deserves further study, although the evidence is not yet sufficient to establish the explanation offered by the original witness.

The correct response is neither immediate belief nor automatic dismissal. It is to determine what was actually observed, under what conditions, and whether the observation can be repeated or independently verified.

The same standard should apply whether the plant is an ordinary roadside herb or a species that has never been encountered outside a sealed chamber beneath the oldest district of the Grand Library.

Unusual organisms do not require less careful study than familiar ones. They require more.

1.7. An unfinished catalogue

No botanical catalogue can claim to represent the entire flora of the Known Realms. Even within well-surveyed regions, small species may be overlooked, seasonal plants may remain dormant for years, and organisms confined to specialised habitats may escape notice. Beyond the established routes of travel lie territories whose vegetation has been described only in fragments.

The collections of the Grand Library are extensive, but their extent should not be confused with completeness. They reflect the history of exploration as much as the distribution of plant life. Some regions have been studied repeatedly because they are accessible or economically important. Others are represented by a handful of specimens collected by travellers who lacked the time or equipment to investigate them properly.

There are also gaps that cannot be filled simply by sending another expedition. Certain plants are difficult to preserve. Others produce flowers or fruits only under particular conditions. Some grow in places where collection is dangerous, prohibited or ecologically irresponsible. In such cases, drawings, photographs, detailed field notes and observations of living populations may provide information that a pressed specimen cannot.

The methods must suit the organism and the question being asked.

A catalogue should also distinguish between a plant that has not been found recently and one that is known to have disappeared. It should separate a species confirmed by examined specimens from one reported only in a traveller’s account. It should record disputed identifications rather than allowing them to pass unnoticed into later editions.

These distinctions may make a catalogue less tidy. They make it more trustworthy.

The work of classification is consequently never finished. Each generation inherits the specimens, descriptions and disagreements of those who came before it. Some conclusions survive scrutiny; others must be revised. New evidence may change the position of a species or reveal that what was once considered a single organism comprises several distinct forms.

There is no shame in such revisions. The greater error would be to preserve an outdated conclusion merely because it has become familiar.

The task of the taxonomist is to give the living world an order that can be examined, questioned and improved. A good classification makes it easier to recognise what is known, to identify what remains uncertain, and to decide where further study is most needed.

It is not a fence placed around nature. It is a means of finding our way through it.

2. A History of Plant Taxonomy in the Realms

The oldest botanical records held by the Grand Library were not written by botanists.

They were written by people who needed to know which plants could be eaten, which would make a fever worse, which roots could be stored through winter, and which trees would provide timber that did not split after a year in the rain. Their observations were rarely concerned with classification as we understand it today. They were concerned with the consequences of getting a plant wrong.

Some of these records are little more than lists. Others are remarkably detailed accounts of harvesting seasons, soil conditions, flowering periods and the preparation of remedies. A few contain illustrations accurate enough to identify species that have since become uncommon. Many cannot be interpreted with confidence, either because the descriptions are too vague or because the names have changed beyond recognition.

Nevertheless, they form the foundation upon which later botanical study was built.

The history of taxonomy is not the history of a single discovery. It is the history of many attempts to organise knowledge, each shaped by the plants available to study, the methods of the period and the questions that scholars considered important.

2.1. Before the Taxonomists

Long before botanical institutes and formal herbaria existed, communities developed detailed knowledge of the plants around them.

Farmers distinguished crops according to their growth, yield and suitability for different soils. Gardeners selected varieties that survived frost or produced larger seeds. Physicians and healers recorded plants used in the treatment of illness, while craftspeople understood which barks yielded dyes, which fibres could be woven, and which woods resisted damp.

Much of this knowledge was transmitted orally. In some communities, it was preserved through songs, seasonal ceremonies, apprenticeships or family records. Other traditions maintained written catalogues, particularly in regions where the cultivation of medicinal gardens had become an established profession.

These systems did not all organise plants in the same way. Some grouped them by use; others by habitat, appearance, season or the parts that were harvested. A coastal community might maintain a detailed distinction between plants growing in salt marshes, plants rooted in dunes and plants found along freshwater streams. To an outsider, the distinctions could appear unnecessarily local. To the people who depended upon those plants, they reflected differences that mattered.

An edible root and a poisonous root might look almost identical to an inexperienced observer, yet the two could grow in different soils or emerge at different times of year. The knowledge required to distinguish them was not merely a matter of naming. It depended upon familiarity with the living plants and the places in which they grew.

The earliest surviving records from the western provinces suggest that some communities also recognised relationships between plants. Several varieties of a cultivated crop might be recorded together, while trees producing similar fruits were grouped in the same section of a household register. Whether these arrangements represented deliberate attempts at classification or simply practical organisation is often impossible to determine.

It would be a mistake, however, to regard these early records as primitive versions of modern scientific catalogues. They were made for different purposes and under different conditions. A system designed to help a community gather food safely need not resemble one intended to reconstruct the evolutionary relationships of thousands of species.

Both can preserve valuable knowledge.

The problem arises when later scholars extract a name or description from its original context and assume that it means precisely what a modern botanical term would mean. An old record must be interpreted according to the evidence it contains, not according to the system we might wish its author had used.

2.2. The Age of the Herbals

As written scholarship spread between the great cities and centres of learning, descriptions of useful plants began to circulate more widely. Collections of botanical knowledge were copied, translated and expanded by generations of scholars. Physicians added observations from their own practice; gardeners recorded cultivated varieties; travellers introduced descriptions of plants unfamiliar to the readers of earlier works.

These books became known in many regions as herbals.

The term covers a wide range of works. Some were primarily medical, arranging plants according to the ailments for which they were prescribed. Others included instructions for cultivation, notes on harvesting and illustrations intended to help readers recognise the species described. A few attempted broader surveys of the vegetation of particular regions.

The usefulness of a herbal depended heavily upon the accuracy of its descriptions. An illustration could be copied repeatedly, preserving a recognisable image long after the original plant had disappeared from the author's region. Unfortunately, the same process could preserve mistakes. An artist working from an earlier drawing might simplify a leaf, alter the arrangement of flowers or omit a feature that distinguished one species from another.

Over several generations of copying, a plant could acquire an appearance that existed only on the page.

The archives contain one particularly troublesome example: a medicinal plant known in three provinces as the silver lantern. The earliest surviving illustration shows a low herb with pale, bell-shaped flowers. Later copies depict it as a climbing vine, and the accompanying descriptions begin to refer to aerial roots that do not appear in the original account. By the time the illustration reached the northern medical colleges, it was being used to identify at least two different species.

The name survived. The identity did not.

Such cases encouraged some scholars to compare written descriptions with living plants whenever possible. Instead of relying exclusively upon inherited texts, they began to examine the organisms themselves, recording details that earlier authors had overlooked. The change was gradual, and it did not make books obsolete. It altered the relationship between written authority and direct observation.

A description could still be useful, but it had to withstand comparison with the plant it claimed to describe.

2.3. The First Great Botanical Collections

The establishment of permanent botanical gardens and organised collections marked an important change in the study of plants. For the first time, scholars in some institutions could compare living specimens gathered from several regions without undertaking the journeys themselves.

The advantages were considerable. Plants could be observed through different stages of growth, cultivated under controlled conditions and compared with species already known to the institution. Seeds could be exchanged between gardens, allowing researchers to study differences in germination and development. Collections also gave students access to organisms that would otherwise have been available only through descriptions or illustrations.

Preserved specimens became equally important.

A living plant may change considerably over the course of a year. Flowers fade, leaves fall, fruits mature and entire stems die back. A pressed specimen, if properly prepared, allows at least some of its structures to be examined long after the plant has been collected. It can be labelled, stored and revisited when a new question arises.

The early herbaria of the Realms were not always well organised. Some consisted of bundles of dried plants kept between sheets of paper, with labels that recorded little more than a place name. Others were private collections whose owners were reluctant to permit examination by strangers. Specimens were sometimes exchanged without adequate documentation, and a plant gathered by one collector could later be attributed to another.

These shortcomings became more serious as collections grew.

A botanical garden might receive seeds from a distant expedition, cultivate the resulting plants for several years and eventually distribute specimens to other institutions. If the original collection site had been recorded incorrectly, the error could spread through every subsequent catalogue. If the plant had been confused with a related species, the cultivated material might acquire an identity that was difficult to challenge once it had been accepted by several respected scholars.

The lesson was not that collections were unreliable. It was that a collection required records as careful as the specimens themselves.

At the Grand Library, the oldest surviving botanical cabinet contains plants gathered during the reign of Queen Maereth III. Several specimens remain identifiable, although their labels refer to administrative boundaries that no longer exist. Others bear names that cannot be matched with any modern description. One packet contains nothing but a fragment of stem and a handwritten note reading:

Found growing from the roof of the western tower. Not planted by us.

The tower itself was demolished two centuries ago.

We cannot establish the identity of the plant from that fragment alone. Its record has nevertheless been preserved, because even incomplete evidence may become useful when considered alongside future discoveries.

2.4. From Lists of Plants to Systems of Classification

As botanical collections expanded, scholars faced a practical problem. The number of known plants had grown beyond what could be managed through separate descriptions and isolated lists.

A botanist studying a new species needed to know whether it had already been described under another name. A curator receiving an unfamiliar specimen needed a reliable way to compare it with existing collections. Students required methods for identifying plants without having to memorise every description in the library.

The answer was not simply to produce longer catalogues. Scholars began developing more systematic ways of grouping plants according to shared characteristics.

Early systems often relied upon conspicuous features: whether a plant was a tree or an herb, whether its leaves were divided or entire, or how its flowers were arranged. These features were comparatively easy to observe and could be useful for identification. They did not always reveal the relationships between plants, however, and different systems sometimes produced very different groupings from the same collection of specimens.

The difficulty lay partly in deciding which characteristics deserved the greatest weight.

Two plants might share the same leaf shape but differ substantially in their flowers and fruits. Another pair might look quite different while sharing important structural features. A system that placed too much emphasis on one visible character could separate plants that belonged together or unite those that did not.

There was also a difference between arranging plants for convenience and arranging them according to their natural relationships. A system based upon flower colour might help a gardener find a particular kind of bloom. It would not necessarily provide a reliable account of how the plants were related.

The distinction became increasingly important as botanists compared collections from distant regions. Features that appeared distinctive in a local flora could prove widespread among unrelated plants elsewhere. Other characteristics, once considered minor, became useful when examined across a larger range of species.

Classification developed through these comparisons. No single observation supplied a complete answer, and no arrangement remained beyond revision simply because it had been widely adopted.

At the Grand Library, the transition can be traced through successive editions of the Register of Known Vegetation. The earliest volumes are organised largely by habitat and practical use. Later editions introduce separate groups based upon morphology, followed by revisions that attempt to account for broader relationships. The older arrangements were not discarded entirely; their information remained valuable for locating plants and understanding how earlier scholars had studied them.

The register grew less simple as it grew more useful.

2.5. The Standardisation of Botanical Names

The expansion of botanical knowledge created another difficulty: the same plant could be described in several places under different names, while a single name could be applied to different plants.

Long descriptive names were once commonly used to distinguish species within a genus. Such names could contain several words describing the plant's appearance or properties. As more species were recorded, these descriptions became cumbersome, especially when scholars needed to compare plants across languages and publications.

The adoption of a consistent two-part scientific naming system helped address the problem. Each species was assigned a name composed of a generic name followed by a specific epithet. This approach became closely associated with the work of the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus, particularly the publication of Species Plantarum in 1753.

The system did not eliminate disagreement over the identity or boundaries of species. It provided a more consistent way to name them and refer to them in scientific writing.

For the scholars of the Realms, the equivalent reform came much later, following a series of disputes between the botanical colleges of the central kingdoms. Each college maintained its own register, and several had established naming conventions that were incompatible with those of their neighbours. An expedition could return with a specimen that was considered a new species in one city and an already known plant in another. The disagreement might concern the plant itself, the name applied to it, or both.

The Council of Botanical Collections was eventually convened to establish common rules for the recording and publication of plant names. Its first code was imperfect. It failed to resolve several existing disputes and left some regional traditions inadequately represented. Nevertheless, it established a shared framework that later revisions could improve.

The current rules require a published name to be accompanied by a description or diagnosis sufficient to distinguish the proposed taxon, together with a designated reference specimen or other permitted basis under the applicable code. Names must also be checked against earlier publications to determine whether they have already been used and whether an older name has priority.

These requirements may seem excessively formal to a collector who has just discovered a remarkable plant. They exist for a reason. A name is not merely a personal label attached to a discovery; it becomes part of a shared scientific record.

A botanist may propose a new name, but the proposal must be supported by evidence and made available to other researchers. Subsequent examination may show that the plant was already known, that its supposed distinguishing characteristics fall within the variation of an existing species, or that the original description included material from more than one organism.

The name may then be rejected, replaced or placed into synonymy.

Such changes can be frustrating, particularly for the person who first described the plant. They are nonetheless essential to a system intended to serve the wider scientific community.

2.6. The Influence of Evolutionary Thought

A classification can describe similarities between organisms without explaining why those similarities exist.

The development of evolutionary theory changed the questions botanists asked of their classifications. Rather than treating groups simply as collections of plants that shared visible features, researchers increasingly sought to understand the historical relationships between species and the processes through which those relationships had arisen.

Some similarities reflected shared ancestry. Others had developed independently as plants adapted to comparable conditions. Distinguishing between these possibilities required careful comparison and, in many cases, evidence that had not been considered important by earlier taxonomists.

The introduction of evolutionary thinking did not make morphology obsolete. It changed how morphological evidence was interpreted. Structures that appeared similar were examined in the context of development, function and ancestry, while differences once considered decisive were sometimes recognised as adaptations to particular environments.

Later methods extended this work. Anatomical studies, reproductive observations and, eventually, molecular evidence gave botanists additional ways to investigate relationships that could not be resolved from external appearance alone. Modern plant taxonomy therefore draws upon multiple sources of evidence rather than relying upon one universal set of visible characters.

The scholars of the Grand Library encountered a comparable shift when several long-established plant groups were reconsidered following the examination of their reproductive development. Species that had been grouped together because of their similar foliage proved to have different histories. Other plants, previously separated because of their growth forms, were found to share characteristics suggesting a closer relationship than their appearance had indicated.

Not every proposed revision was accepted. Some were based upon too few specimens; others depended upon assumptions that later observations failed to support. Disagreement was unavoidable, but the arguments increasingly centred upon evidence that other scholars could examine.

A classification became less a monument to the judgement of its author and more a working account of the relationships that the evidence supported.

This was a considerable improvement, though it did not resolve every difficulty. Evolutionary relationships can be difficult to reconstruct when species have diverged rapidly, when hybridisation has occurred, or when the available specimens are incomplete. Different methods may produce conflicting results, and taxonomists must decide which interpretation best accounts for the evidence.

The existence of such disagreements does not make classification arbitrary. It makes the quality of the evidence, and the reasoning applied to it, especially important.

2.7. The Botanical History of the Known Realms

The history of botanical study within the Realms has followed no single path.

The southern kingdoms developed extensive records of cultivated plants, partly because their agricultural institutions maintained seed houses and experimental gardens. The northern territories preserved a different body of knowledge, much of it concerned with plants able to survive short growing seasons, prolonged frost and poor soils. Coastal settlements accumulated detailed records of salt-tolerant vegetation, while the communities of the great forests maintained knowledge of species that could not readily be cultivated outside their native habitats.

These traditions did not remain separate. Trade carried seeds and dried specimens between regions. Physicians exchanged recipes, gardeners requested unusual varieties, and travelling scholars copied descriptions into their notebooks. The same plant might be known through several independent traditions before its identity was recognised across the wider botanical community.

The establishment of the Grand Library's Department of Botany brought many of these records together, although not without difficulty. Early curators attempted to arrange the collections according to the classification most widely accepted in the central kingdoms. In doing so, they sometimes discarded local names that appeared redundant or entered unfamiliar species under the closest available heading.

Some of these decisions were later reversed.

A name that seemed to refer to several different plants occasionally proved to describe one species with a broad geographical range. In other cases, a supposedly universal name concealed several species that local collectors had long distinguished from one another. The process of bringing collections together revealed both the value of standardisation and the danger of assuming that a single institution possessed all the knowledge necessary to interpret them.

The Department's later catalogues therefore adopted a more cautious approach. Local names were retained wherever their provenance could be established. Earlier identifications were cross-referenced rather than silently erased. Disputed records were marked for further examination, and specimens that could not be identified with confidence were preserved separately until additional evidence became available.

This approach has made the catalogues more complicated. It has also made them more honest.

The history of the department contains its share of celebrated expeditions and important discoveries, but its progress has depended just as much upon patient work performed far from the field. Curators compared labels, translators recovered the meanings of obsolete names, illustrators corrected inaccurate plates, and generations of assistants transferred fragile specimens into better storage.

Their contributions rarely appear in accounts of great discoveries. Without them, many discoveries would have remained unusable.

2.8. The Unfinished History

The history of taxonomy is often presented as a succession of improvements, each replacing an inferior system with a better one. This description is convenient, but it overlooks the extent to which earlier methods remain useful for particular purposes.

A classification based upon habitat may still be the best way to organise a field survey. A traditional herbal may preserve practical knowledge that is absent from a modern taxonomic treatment. A historical name may be indispensable when tracing the movement of a cultivated plant through trade records. None of these sources needs to be mistaken for a modern classification in order to be valuable.

The task is to understand what each source can establish, what it cannot establish, and how it should be compared with other evidence.

Nor should the history of taxonomy be considered separate from the history of the plants themselves. Species disappear from their former habitats. Cultivated varieties escape into the wild. New populations are discovered in places that were once thought botanically unremarkable. Human activity changes the distribution of plants, sometimes making older descriptions difficult to interpret.

The catalogue must change accordingly.

The Grand Library retains obsolete classifications because they document the development of botanical thought. It preserves rejected names because they explain the history of identification. It keeps incomplete specimens because future researchers may find information in them that earlier scholars could not recognise.

These records are not evidence of failure. They are evidence of work still in progress.

When I began my studies, I imagined that sufficient reading and careful observation would eventually make the botanical literature manageable. I no longer hold that belief. Every collection I examine seems to lead to another set of records, another disagreement or another plant whose identity remains uncertain. The literature expands because the world continues to present questions that earlier scholars did not know to ask.

I have come to regard this not as an inconvenience, but as one of the reasons the discipline remains worth pursuing.

A classification should preserve what has been learned without pretending that learning has ended. Its history should remind us that even well-supported conclusions can be revised, that practical knowledge may precede formal explanation by centuries, and that the most useful contribution a scholar can make is sometimes to identify a mistake that has been repeated for generations.

The botanist who studies an old catalogue should therefore ask two questions. What did its author know? And what could the author not have known?

The answers are rarely the same.

3. Principles of Botanical Identification

The first plant I was asked to identify without assistance was a small herb growing beside a stone irrigation channel in the southern provinces. I was nineteen years old and sufficiently confident in my education to believe that the exercise would take no more than a few minutes.

The leaves were narrow, the stem slightly reddish, and the flowers had begun to open along one side of the upper stalk. I recognised a resemblance to a species I had studied the previous winter and wrote its name in my notebook.

My instructor examined the specimen, asked me to describe the arrangement of its leaves, and then requested that I look at the base of the stem.

I had not noticed that the leaves near the ground were arranged differently from those higher up. Nor had I examined the small structures surrounding the flowers. The plant was not the species I had named, although the resemblance was sufficient to explain my mistake.

My instructor made no further comment. She simply returned the specimen to me and suggested that I begin again.

I remember the incident because it was the first time I understood how little the act of recognising a plant had to do with identifying it correctly.

An experienced botanist does not necessarily see more than an inexperienced observer. Rather, the experienced botanist has learned what to look for, which details are likely to matter, and when an apparent resemblance should be treated with suspicion.

Identification is a skill developed through observation and comparison. It requires knowledge of plant structure, familiarity with the available literature and a willingness to revise an initial conclusion when the evidence demands it.

The principles described in this chapter apply to the identification of familiar plants and unfamiliar specimens alike. They should also be followed when examining organisms whose characteristics fall outside the forms commonly encountered in the field.

3.1. Begin with the Specimen, Not the Name

When presented with an unfamiliar plant, the observer should resist the temptation to assign it a name immediately.

Names encourage association. A plant with silver leaves may recall another silver-leaved species; a flower with a particular shape may resemble a familiar medicinal herb. Such associations are useful starting points, but they can also influence what the observer notices next.

Once a botanist believes that a specimen belongs to a particular species, there is a tendency to interpret subsequent observations in support of that conclusion. Differences may be dismissed as ordinary variation, while similarities receive disproportionate attention.

The safest approach is to begin with a description of the specimen itself.

Record what can be observed before deciding what those observations mean. Is the stem upright, creeping or climbing? Are the leaves arranged alternately along the stem, opposite one another, or in groups around a single point? Are the flowers solitary or gathered into an inflorescence? Is the surface smooth, hairy, waxy or covered in small projections?

These observations do not need to be elegant. They need to be accurate.

A description such as a peculiar plant with unusual leaves has little value to another botanist. A description noting that the leaves are narrow, lance-shaped, arranged in opposite pairs and covered with fine hairs along their lower surfaces provides information that can be checked against a specimen.

The distinction becomes particularly important when the plant cannot be examined again. A collector may spend several days travelling through a remote region before returning to a specimen that requires further study. If the original notes contain only an impression of its appearance, much of the available evidence may have been lost.

Field observations should therefore be recorded while the plant is still accessible. Measurements, sketches and written descriptions often preserve details that are difficult to reconstruct from memory.

The observer should also distinguish between direct observations and interpretations.

Consider the following entries from a hypothetical field notebook:

Observed: the upper leaves bend towards the eastern wall of the chamber.

Interpretation: the plant grows towards the eastern wall.

The first statement describes what was seen at a particular moment. The second proposes an explanation for the observed position. Further examination would be needed to establish whether the plant consistently grows in that direction, whether the apparent movement is related to light, or whether the leaves simply developed in that position.

This distinction is not a matter of excessive caution. It allows another researcher to assess the evidence without first having to separate observation from the collector's assumptions.

3.2. Examine the Whole Plant

A plant is more than its most conspicuous feature.

Flowers attract attention, particularly when their colour or shape is unusual. Leaves are often easier to examine and remain available for much of the growing season. Yet neither flowers nor leaves should be considered in isolation when other parts of the plant can be studied.

The structures available for identification depend upon the organism and its stage of development. A mature flowering plant may provide leaves, stems, flowers and fruits. A young seedling may possess only a few leaves and an undeveloped root system. A tree examined in winter may have no leaves at all, but its buds, bark, branching pattern and reproductive structures may still offer useful evidence.

The botanist must work with what is available while recognising the limitations of incomplete material.

Roots

Roots are often overlooked in field descriptions because they are difficult to examine without disturbing the plant. Nevertheless, their form can provide valuable information.

Some plants possess a prominent primary root from which smaller lateral roots develop. Others produce numerous roots of similar size, forming a more diffuse system. Certain species develop thickened storage roots, while others produce specialised structures associated with climbing, attachment or survival in particular environments.

Roots may also show adaptations to the conditions in which a plant grows. Plants living in waterlogged soils, for example, may possess structures that assist gas exchange. Those growing in dry environments may develop extensive root systems that allow them to obtain water from a larger volume of soil.

Such characteristics can assist identification, but they must be interpreted carefully. Root form may change with age, soil conditions and the availability of water. A specimen grown in a botanical garden may not display exactly the same root development as a plant growing in its native habitat.

Where roots cannot be collected, their appearance and surroundings should be documented whenever possible.

Stems

The stem provides information about the plant's growth form and the arrangement of its other structures.

It may be herbaceous and relatively soft, or woody and capable of supporting the plant over many years. It may grow upright, spread across the ground, climb through surrounding vegetation or remain largely underground.

The arrangement of branches, the presence of nodes and internodes, and the texture of the stem may all be useful. Some species possess ridges, grooves, hairs or glands. Others develop thorns or prickles, although these structures should be described precisely rather than grouped together merely because they appear sharp.

The observer should also note whether the stem changes along its length. Young growth may differ in colour or texture from older sections, and some plants produce specialised shoots only during particular seasons.

Leaves

Leaves provide a particularly rich source of identifying characters.

Their arrangement along the stem is often among the first features to examine. Leaves may be alternate, opposite or whorled. Their blades may be simple or divided into separate leaflets, and their outlines range from nearly circular to extremely narrow.

The margin may be smooth, toothed, serrated or lobed. The tip and base may have distinctive shapes, while the venation may be parallel, branching or arranged in a network.

Other useful features include the presence of hairs, the texture of the surface, the thickness of the blade and the relationship between the upper and lower surfaces.

These details should be observed on several leaves where possible. A leaf damaged by insects or distorted by poor growing conditions may not represent the usual form of the species.

Nor should every difference be treated as taxonomically significant. Leaves from the base of a plant may differ from those near its flowers. Young leaves may have a different shape from mature ones, and leaves growing in deep shade may be larger or thinner than those exposed to direct sunlight.

A reliable description accounts for these differences rather than selecting whichever leaf most closely resembles an illustration.

Flowers and Reproductive Structures

In flowering plants, the reproductive structures frequently provide characters essential for distinguishing closely related species.

The number and arrangement of floral parts, the shape of the calyx and corolla, the position of the ovary, and the structure of the stamens and stigma may all be important. In some groups, distinctions between species depend upon details that cannot be seen clearly without magnification.

The arrangement of flowers must also be recorded. A single flower at the end of a stem is structurally different from a cluster of flowers, even when both arrangements create a similar impression from a distance.

Flowers should be examined at an appropriate stage of development. Buds may not yet display their mature form, while old flowers may have lost petals or undergone changes following pollination.

Where several flowers are available, the observer should compare them rather than assuming that the first examined is typical.

Fruits and Seeds

Fruits and seeds can be particularly useful when flowers are absent or when related species possess similar blossoms.

The botanist should note the type of fruit, its shape, dimensions, surface and method of opening, where applicable. The number and arrangement of seeds may also be diagnostic.

The distinction between a fruit and a seed is important. In flowering plants, the fruit develops from the ovary after fertilisation, while the seeds develop from ovules. Their structures and development provide different kinds of evidence.

Fruits should be examined at maturity whenever possible. Immature fruits may differ substantially in colour, texture and shape, and some structures change during drying.

A specimen collected at the wrong stage may therefore be difficult to identify even when its species is well known.

No single part of the plant is guaranteed to provide the answer. Identification depends upon assembling the available evidence and determining which combination of characters most reliably distinguishes the specimen from related organisms.

4. The Taxonomic Hierarchy

THE LIVING INDEX · VOLUME I: PRINCIPLES AND METHODS

A specimen arrives at the Grand Library without a label. It is a flowering plant, collected somewhere in the eastern territories, though the accompanying notes provide no more precise location. Its leaves are narrow and leathery, its stem woody at the base, and its flowers arranged in clusters at the ends of the branches.

The first question is not necessarily its species.

Perhaps the specimen can be assigned to a familiar family. Perhaps its reproductive structures allow a more precise identification, placing it within a particular genus. Only after comparison with the relevant descriptions and reference specimens might it be possible to establish the species.

Each conclusion narrows the field of possibilities. Each depends upon the evidence supporting the previous one.

This is the purpose of the taxonomic hierarchy: to arrange organisms into nested groups, from broad categories containing many kinds of plants to narrower groups representing increasingly specific sets of organisms.

The hierarchy is not merely a convenient filing system. Modern classifications aim to reflect relationships between organisms, using available morphological, anatomical, developmental and molecular evidence. Group boundaries may be revised when new evidence becomes available, but the hierarchy provides a shared framework in which those revisions can be recorded.

5.1. Understanding the hierarchy

The principal taxonomic ranks traditionally used in botanical classification, from broadest to narrowest, are:

  1. Kingdom
  2. Division
  3. Class
  4. Order
  5. Family
  6. Genus
  7. Species

In botanical literature, division is traditionally used where other branches of biology often use phylum. Both terms refer to the same general rank. Intermediate ranks, such as subclass, subfamily and subgenus, may be used when a more detailed classification is required.

A taxonomic rank is a category. A taxon is the actual group assigned to that category. Family, for example, is a rank; a particular named family is a taxon. The name of a group, the category to which it belongs and the organisms included within it are related, but they are not interchangeable.

5.2. Kingdom

The kingdom is one of the broadest traditional ranks in biological classification. It gathers large groups of organisms that share fundamental characteristics and occupy distinct positions within the wider classification of life.

In a conventional botanical classification, plants are assigned to the kingdom Plantae. The exact limits of the plant kingdom depend upon the classification system being used, particularly when algae and historically associated groups are considered. For this volume, the term refers to the plant kingdom adopted by the Grand Library's current botanical catalogue.

A small annual herb, a flowering tree, a fern and a moss differ considerably in structure and life cycle, yet they can all be placed within Plantae under the classification used here. The rank is too broad to identify most individual specimens; its importance lies in establishing their broadest placement.

Within the Known Realms, the Department of Botany also maintains separate catalogues for organisms that resemble plants but whose biological classification remains disputed. A stationary organism is not necessarily a plant, nor does the possession of leaf-like structures establish membership of Plantae. Such cases require examination rather than assumption.

5.3. Division

A division separates the kingdom into broader groups of plants that share important characteristics of structure, reproduction or evolutionary history. The rank is useful for distinguishing major groups that differ in fundamental biology: flowering plants, conifers, ferns and mosses, for example, have different reproductive structures and life cycles.

Traditional plant divisions have changed over time, and modern classifications do not always organise these groups in precisely the same way. A botanist should consult the classification adopted by the relevant catalogue rather than assume that every historical division remains universally accepted.

In the Grand Library's archives, older specimens sometimes bear division names no longer used in the current register. Such labels should not be altered without preserving the original determination. Historical names can help trace how a classification developed. A division contains classes, each of which contains orders, and so on down the hierarchy.

5.4. Class

A class groups related orders within a broader taxonomic unit. The classification reflects relationships that extend beyond the features used to identify an individual family or genus. Organisms within one class may differ considerably in appearance, size and habitat while sharing a position within a wider classification.

A common mistake is to assume that every member of a class must closely resemble every other member. This is not the purpose of the rank. A class is a broad grouping, not a description of a single physical form. Nor do taxonomic ranks guarantee that their groups contain equal numbers of organisms or equivalent levels of diversity.

For practical identification, botanists often work at lower ranks. Classes become particularly useful when organising large collections, comparing major groups or studying the broader history of plant diversity.

5.5. Order

An order brings together related families. It allows botanists to recognise broader relationships that may not be immediately apparent from individual species. Two families may contain plants with different growth forms and habitats while sharing characteristics that support their placement within the same order.

Orders are useful in research and collection management. Higher-level groupings allow related families to be considered together and help researchers navigate large bodies of information.

In botanical nomenclature, names of orders commonly end in -ales. This ending is a useful clue when reading scientific literature, but the name's rank should be checked whenever there is doubt. An ending alone does not prove that a proposed classification is correct; it indicates the rank at which the name is used under the relevant rules.

5.6. Family

The family is one of the most useful ranks for the working botanist. A family contains one or more genera grouped on the basis of shared characteristics and relationships. Many families can be recognised through combinations of features involving flowers, fruits, leaves or other structures. Familiarity with these patterns can greatly narrow the possibilities when identifying an unknown specimen.

A family may contain herbs, shrubs, climbers and trees. Its members may occupy different habitats and vary considerably in appearance. What unites them is not a requirement to look alike, but the evidence supporting their placement within the same taxonomic group.

In the botanical naming system used for plants, family names commonly end in -aceae. Real-world examples include Rosaceae, the rose family, and Fabaceae, the legume family.

The Grand Library uses the family as an important organisational level within its reference collection. Researchers unfamiliar with a species can begin by establishing its family and consulting the descriptions, illustrations and identification keys available for that group. Even if flowers or fruits are missing, family-level placement may provide a meaningful starting point.

Family resemblance should not be treated as proof that every member possesses the same medicinal, ecological or agricultural properties. Relatedness can suggest questions for further investigation, but it does not replace evidence about an individual species.

5.7. Genus

A genus groups closely related species within a single taxonomic unit. Species belonging to the same genus may share a distinctive combination of structural characteristics, although their appearance can vary considerably.

The limits of a genus are not always straightforward. Taxonomists may disagree about whether a group should be divided into several genera or retained as one larger genus. Such disagreements should be resolved through examination of evidence, not by counting species or judging how convenient a name may be.

Genus names form the first part of a plant's scientific name. They begin with a capital letter and are conventionally italicised in print. In the real botanical name Quercus robur, Quercus is the genus, while the second word identifies the species within it.

Within the Grand Library's collections, genus-level catalogues provide a convenient place to compare descriptions, illustrations and specimens of related species. If the evidence establishes the genus but not the species, the record should reflect that limit rather than supply an unsupported specific name.

5.8. Species

The species is the basic unit of the principal taxonomic hierarchy. In everyday language, the word may suggest a clearly defined kind of plant whose members all look alike. In practice, species can be difficult to delimit. Individuals within a species may differ considerably, while separate species can share many characteristics.

There is no single species definition that resolves every case. The ability to reproduce with one another is relevant to many organisms, but it is not a universal test for plants. Some plants reproduce without fertilisation; others hybridise with related species, and some are known only from incomplete or preserved material.

Botanists therefore use combinations of evidence to determine whether a group should be treated as a species. These may include morphology, reproductive biology, genetics, ecological differences and evolutionary relationships. The appropriate evidence varies between groups.

Consider two plants found along the same river. Both have narrow leaves and clusters of pale flowers. One produces small, dry fruits that split at maturity; the other produces fleshy fruits containing several seeds. If these differences remain consistent across sufficient specimens and are supported by other evidence, they may help establish that the plants represent separate species. The conclusion cannot rest on fruit alone, but the example shows how several characters combine to distinguish related organisms.

A species name does not guarantee that every individual will display every characteristic described for it. Descriptions are based on the observed range of variation and may need revision when new populations are studied. A taxonomist should consider both typical characteristics and the limits of variation documented within a species.

5.9. Ranks below species

The principal hierarchy does not end when a species has been identified. Some species contain recognisable groups of populations that differ consistently. When those differences are considered taxonomically significant, botanists may recognise ranks below species, such as subspecies, variety or form.

These categories are not interchangeable, and their use depends upon the nature of the differences and the conventions adopted by the relevant taxonomic treatment. A subspecies may be recognised when a distinct population possesses consistent characteristics; a variety may identify a recognised group with particular distinguishing features. A form is often used for a more limited variation, although practices differ among groups and traditions.

Such ranks should not be created merely because two plants look slightly different. A difference in colour, for example, may reflect ordinary variation, environmental conditions or a temporary developmental stage. Before assigning a rank, the botanist must determine whether the difference is consistent and whether recognising it contributes meaningfully to the classification.

The Grand Library's catalogue includes older cases in which separate varieties were later found to fall within the ordinary variation of a single species. Other cases required the opposite correction: what had been treated as one variable species was found to contain distinct populations deserving further recognition. Classification is a process of evaluating evidence, not merely adding levels to the hierarchy.

5.10. Taxa, ranks and the problem of boundaries

A taxon is a recognised group of organisms at any rank. A particular named genus is a taxon; so is a particular named species. The rank indicates the category assigned to the group, while the taxon is the group itself.

Suppose a genus is divided into two genera after new evidence shows that its species belong to separate evolutionary lineages. The species may remain recognised as distinct, but their placement in the hierarchy changes. Their names may also require revision to reflect their new generic assignments.

Alternatively, two genera may be united when the evidence supports treating them as a single group. Names and catalogue records may need updating, while the original publications and identifications remain part of the historical record.

Such changes can be disruptive for libraries and researchers. Indexes must be revised, specimens relabelled or annotated, and older literature cross-referenced. Yet maintaining an accurate classification is preferable to preserving a structure that no longer reflects the best available evidence.

The ranks themselves also have limitations. A family does not necessarily contain the same number of genera as another family, nor does a genus always represent the same degree of diversity as every other genus. The hierarchy is a system of nested categories, not a scale in which every level contains equivalent quantities of biological difference.

Rank should not be treated as a measure of importance. A species is not less worthy of study because it belongs to a small genus, and a large family is not necessarily more significant than a small one. Classification clarifies relationships; it does not establish a hierarchy of value.

5.12. The hierarchy as a working tool

The taxonomic hierarchy is often taught as a sequence to memorise. Knowing the ranks in the correct order is useful, but memorisation alone does not explain why the system matters.

For the botanist in the field, the hierarchy provides a way to express the level of certainty reached during identification. A specimen may be recognisable as a member of a family even when its genus remains uncertain. A genus may be established while the species cannot be determined because necessary reproductive structures are missing.

For the curator, the hierarchy helps organise collections and link related records. For the researcher, it allows observations about one species to be compared with evidence from related groups. For the taxonomist, it provides a structure that can be revised as understanding improves.

A classification should not be treated as permanent merely because it is neatly arranged. Nor should it be dismissed as arbitrary because its boundaries sometimes change. The hierarchy is a tool for expressing relationships, and its quality depends upon the evidence and reasoning used to establish them.

The ranks give us a language in which to discuss the organisation of plant diversity. They do not remove the need to examine the plants themselves. When a specimen reaches the Department of Botany, the first task is to determine what the available evidence supports. The botanist may arrive at a species, a genus, a family or a broader group. Each can be a useful result, provided the conclusion is recorded honestly.

It is better to know the limits of an identification than to possess a name that cannot be defended.

5. Nomenclature and Scientific Names

On the names by which plants are known, recorded, and sometimes misunderstood.

A botanist returning from a distant province may bring back a specimen known locally as the widow's bell. A physician in another kingdom may recognise the same plant under the name mourning lily, while a gardener in the southern islands calls it night's cup. Each name may be perfectly meaningful within its own community. The difficulty begins when the three descriptions arrive at the Grand Library and are entered into separate catalogues as though they referred to three different plants.

The reverse problem is equally common. Two villages may use the same name for plants that are not closely related, do not share the same properties, and cannot safely be used in the same way. A name that seems perfectly clear to a local herbalist may become dangerously ambiguous when copied into a medical register hundreds of miles away.

This is why botanical science requires a system of naming that does not depend entirely upon language, geography, or custom. Scientific names provide a shared reference. They allow researchers to compare specimens collected in different regions, follow the history of a discovery, identify mistakes in older publications, and distinguish between plants that happen to look alike.

They do not replace local names. Those names often preserve information that a formal catalogue would otherwise lose: the season in which a plant appears, the animal that feeds upon it, the place where it grows, or the role it has played in the life of a community. The Grand Library therefore records vernacular names wherever reliable information is available. They are treated as part of a plant's history, not as substitutes for its scientific identity.

A scientific name is not necessarily more descriptive, more beautiful, or more truthful than a common one. Its principal advantage is that it can be tied to a defined taxonomic concept and to the evidence supporting that concept.

6.1. The Structure of a Scientific Name

In botanical nomenclature, the standard scientific name of a species is a binomial: two words that together identify the species within a genus. The first word is the genus, and the second is the specific epithet. The complete name consists of both; the epithet by itself is not the full name of the species.

Consider the familiar botanical example Quercus robur, the English oak. Quercus is the genus; robur is the specific epithet. The name is written in italics, with the genus beginning with a capital letter and the epithet beginning with a lowercase letter.

The same format is used for the plants of the Known Realms. A species recorded in the eastern glass marshes might be named Vitraluma palustris: Vitraluma identifies its genus, while palustris distinguishes the species within that genus. The name is invented for this book, but its structure follows the conventions used in real botanical nomenclature.

Once a scientific name has been introduced clearly in a text, the genus may sometimes be abbreviated, provided there is no risk of confusion. Quercus robur may become Q. robur in a section devoted to oaks. This is a convenience, not a different name. In a catalogue containing several genera beginning with the same letter, the full name should be restored.

The specific epithet may refer to a place, a physical feature, a person, a habitat, or another characteristic chosen by the author. It may even be a word whose meaning has become obscure over time. It does not have to provide a reliable description of the plant. A plant named alba is not guaranteed to be white; one named minor is not necessarily the smallest member of its genus. A name is a label attached to a taxonomic concept, not a complete description of its subject.

Scientific names are normally italicised in printed botanical works. The names of families, orders, and higher taxonomic groups are not italicised under standard practice. Thus Rosaceae is the name of a family, while Rosa canina is the scientific name of a species. Consistency matters in a library containing millions of records: a catalogue must make it possible to tell whether a word identifies a species, a broader group, a person, or a title cited in the literature.

6.2. The Rules Behind the Names

Scientific names are not assigned according to personal preference alone. Botanical nomenclature follows an internationally recognised set of rules intended to maintain stability and make names traceable to published scientific work. The principal framework used by botanists is the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants, commonly abbreviated to the ICN.

The Code does not decide every question about how plants are related. Classification is a scientific interpretation, and new evidence may lead researchers to reorganise genera or families. Nomenclature provides the rules for naming the groups recognised by that classification.

In general, a new plant name must be effectively published, must follow the relevant formal requirements, and must be connected to a nomenclatural type. Under the modern Code, a new species name is ordinarily published with a description or diagnosis that distinguishes it from related taxa, or with an appropriate reference to one. Requirements have changed over time, so historical publications must be judged according to the rules applicable to them.

Discovering an unfamiliar plant does not automatically establish a new species, and inventing a Latin-sounding name does not make that name scientifically valid. The specimen must be studied, compared with known plants, documented, and published according to the applicable rules. The Department of Botany maintains a register of proposed names from its own expeditions, but a proposed entry is not treated as an established name merely because it appears in the register.

The rules are not infallible, nor do they prevent disagreement. They do, however, provide a common procedure for resolving many disputes and a record through which later researchers can retrace the decisions of their predecessors.

6.3. The Type Specimen

Every accepted botanical name is anchored to a nomenclatural type. For a species, this is generally a preserved specimen, although the rules allow other kinds of material in certain circumstances. The type is not necessarily the most typical, complete, or beautiful example of the species. Its purpose is more precise: it fixes the application of the name.

Suppose three botanists disagree about a plant collected in the western highlands. One believes it belongs to an established species; another considers it a variety of a related plant; the third proposes that it is entirely new. Their disagreement may involve leaf shape, flower structure, habitat, or evolutionary relationships. The type specimen does not settle every biological question, but it establishes the material to which the name is attached.

At the Grand Library, type specimens are stored under controlled conditions and accompanied by detailed records. These include the collector's name, collection number where available, date, locality, habitat notes, and the publication in which the name was established. A specimen collected before reliable maps existed may have a vague locality such as “beyond the northern pass.” Such records are not discarded for being imperfect; their limitations are documented.

The Library distinguishes carefully between types and other important specimens. A later collection may show the plant in flower, reveal its roots, or provide a better example of its usual form. It may be more useful for teaching and identification than the type itself. That does not make it the type.

I once heard a visiting collector insist that his specimen should replace an old type because the old material had lost most of its petals. He had misunderstood the purpose of the collection. A type is not awarded to the specimen that best represents a species in the eyes of the current generation.

Where original material has been lost or destroyed, formal procedures may allow a replacement type to be selected from suitable material. Such decisions require documentation and must follow the nomenclatural rules. A missing specimen is a serious problem, not an invitation to choose whichever example happens to support a preferred theory.

For magical or otherwise unusual plants, type material presents additional difficulties. A specimen may lose its glow, cease producing a scent, or become inactive once removed from its habitat. If these features are relevant to identification, the collector must record them carefully while the plant is alive. Drawings, measurements, photographs, and descriptions may preserve observations that a pressed specimen cannot. The record should distinguish what was observed directly from what was reported by another person.

6.4. Synonyms: When One Plant Has Several Names

A taxonomic synonym is a scientific name that refers to the same taxon as another name under a particular classification. Synonyms arise for several reasons. A species may be described independently by two researchers who do not know of each other's work. A plant originally placed in one genus may later be transferred to another. Or a new study may show that two supposedly distinct species are better treated as one.

Imagine that a botanist publishes the name Lunaria noctiflora for a plant found in the eastern marshes. Several decades later, another researcher examines its reproductive structures and concludes that it belongs in the genus Noctiluma. Under the appropriate nomenclatural rules, the species may be published under a new combination: Noctiluma noctiflora.

The old name does not simply vanish from the record. It remains important because it appears in earlier books, field notes, medicinal recipes, and museum labels. A researcher who searches only for the current name could miss a century of observations.

It is useful to distinguish nomenclatural synonyms from taxonomic synonyms. Nomenclatural synonyms are based on the same type; taxonomic synonyms have different types that a taxonomist considers to belong to the same taxon. A name marked as a synonym should not be deleted as though it were a spelling error. It may be the key to finding an old specimen or understanding why two publications appear to describe different plants.

Common names are not scientific synonyms. Local names are not governed by the same formal rules. Their meanings can shift between villages and generations, and the same phrase may refer to different plants in different regions. A catalogue must distinguish between a documented vernacular name and a scientific name with a formal nomenclatural history.

6.5. Priority, Publication, and Disputed Names

One principal aim of botanical nomenclature is to avoid giving several competing names equal standing for the same taxon. The principle of priority generally favours the earliest legitimate name that applies under the Code, subject to specific exceptions designed to preserve stability.

Priority does not mean that the oldest name ever written in any language must always be used. A farmer's account from three hundred years ago may contain the earliest known description of a plant, but a vernacular phrase in an unpublished diary does not automatically qualify as a scientific name under modern nomenclatural rules. The relevant question is whether a name was established according to the requirements that applied at the time.

Consider a hypothetical dispute concerning Aurea silvatica, a plant collected in the forests of the northern kingdoms. An old expedition journal refers to it as Aurea montana, but the journal contains no formal description. The earlier wording may be historically valuable, yet it does not necessarily take priority as a scientific name. If another botanist discovers that the plant was formally described under a different legitimate name several years before Aurea silvatica was published, the earlier name may have priority, unless an applicable rule or conservation measure changes the outcome.

This work is rarely as dramatic as the expedition that first found the plant. It is often a matter of comparing a printed page with a handwritten label, checking a publication date, or determining whether two specimens really belong to the same species. Yet such work prevents errors from spreading through later literature.

I have known researchers to treat the choice of a name as a matter of pride. They defend a name because it appears in their own monograph or because a respected predecessor established it. Neither argument is sufficient. Names should be maintained when the rules and evidence support them, not because abandoning them would be inconvenient.

At the same time, changing names casually can make scientific communication needlessly difficult. A taxonomic revision should explain what has changed, why it has changed, and which earlier names are affected. Stability is valuable, but it is not the same as refusing to correct a mistake.

6.6. Names That Must Be Corrected

Not every problem with a scientific name involves a major taxonomic dispute. Some are smaller, though no less troublesome in a large collection. A name may be misspelled when copied from an old label. A printer may omit a letter. An author may use a name that has already been assigned to another taxon at the same rank. Two catalogues may record different spellings of what is intended to be the same name.

The first task is to determine what kind of problem has occurred. A simple transcription error should not be treated as the publication of a new species. Equally, an unfamiliar spelling should not be corrected merely because it looks awkward to a modern reader. Historical names must be examined in their original context and according to the rules that govern them.

The Grand Library preserves the wording of original labels whenever possible. Corrections are entered separately, with an explanation of the change and a reference to the evidence supporting it. This allows a researcher to search under a corrected name without losing the ability to locate the original record.

Names may also be illegitimate under the nomenclatural rules, even when they were published in good faith. One possible reason is that the same name had already been used for another taxon of the same rank. Such a conflict is known as homonymy. These distinctions matter because a name can be familiar, widely printed, and still not be the correct name to use under the rules. Popularity alone does not settle nomenclature.

The Department's policy is to avoid silently replacing a name. When a correction affects a widely used entry, the catalogue records the former name, the accepted name, and the reason for the change. Researchers should be able to follow the trail rather than being asked to trust an unexplained alteration.

6.7. Naming Plants of the Known Realms

The flora of the Known Realms presents challenges that do not always arise in ordinary botanical collections. Some plants grow in places that are difficult to revisit; others alter their appearance with the seasons, the surrounding soil, or the presence of particular organisms. A small number exhibit unusual properties attributed to magic, although the nature and consistency of those properties remain subjects of investigation.

None of these circumstances removes the need for careful nomenclature. A plant that glows in darkness should not be named as though it were a source of light unless that feature has been observed and is useful for distinguishing it. A species said to remember the voices of those who speak near it should not receive a scientific description that treats the claim as established fact. The name may refer to a local tradition, but the catalogue must distinguish the tradition from the observation.

Grand Library Herbarium · Illustrative Entry

Vitraluma palustris

Common names: Glass-marsh lantern; fenlight

Recorded habitat: Shallow marshes and seasonally flooded woodland along the eastern lowlands.

Diagnostic features: Narrow leaves with translucent margins; solitary flowers; pale blue luminescence reported after dusk.

Taxonomic status: Provisional species concept within the invented genus Vitraluma.

This is an original species created for the present volume, not a real botanical taxon. Its entry demonstrates how observations, local names, and taxonomic decisions should be recorded separately.

The entry does not claim that luminescence is a magical property. It records the phenomenon and leaves its cause open to investigation. If future observations establish that the light is produced by a symbiotic organism, a chemical reaction, or an effect associated with magic, the description can be expanded without necessarily changing the name.

Nor does the name Vitraluma palustris establish that every plant currently called a glass-marsh lantern belongs to the same species. That question depends on comparisons between specimens, not on the popularity of the vernacular name.

A collector may write Vitraluma sp. when the genus is reasonably established but the species cannot yet be identified. If even the genus is uncertain, a descriptive field designation may be preferable until further comparison is possible. Such labels are useful working tools, but they should not be mistaken for formally published scientific names.

The same care applies to plants that are difficult or dangerous to collect. Where a complete specimen cannot be obtained, the researcher should explain why, preserve whatever material can safely be gathered, and document the missing features. A dramatic account of an encounter is not a substitute for botanical evidence, however remarkable the encounter may have been.

The purpose of nomenclature is not to make every discovery appear settled. It is to allow discoveries to enter a shared record in a form that others can examine, question, and improve.

6.8. The Responsibility of the Catalogue

A scientific name can outlive the circumstances in which it was established. It may pass from an expedition journal into a herbarium register, from the register into a medical handbook, and from the handbook into a farming manual. Each new use gives the name another opportunity to be copied correctly—or incorrectly.

For this reason, the Grand Library treats nomenclature as an ongoing responsibility rather than a task completed when a specimen first receives its label. Every major taxonomic revision must be reflected in the relevant catalogue records. Synonyms should be linked to the accepted name where the relationship is supported. Historical spellings must remain searchable when they occur in important sources. Uncertain identifications should not be upgraded simply because an old entry has been repeated often enough to appear authoritative.

The Library also records the date on which a taxonomic decision was adopted and, where appropriate, the authority responsible for it. A name accepted in an older edition of a flora may have been replaced by a later treatment. Both records can be useful, provided their relationship is clear.

This work requires cooperation between taxonomists, librarians, archivists, conservators, and the specialists responsible for maintaining living collections. A botanist may recognise that two names refer to the same species, but the archivist may discover the original publication. A librarian may identify a catalogue entry copied incorrectly for generations. A conservator may recover enough information from a damaged label to connect a specimen with its original field notes. No single specialist holds all the evidence.

It is tempting to imagine a perfect catalogue in which every plant has one name, every name has one clear meaning, and every disagreement has been resolved. Such a catalogue would be convenient. It would also be misleading. Classification changes because knowledge changes, and the record must accommodate that fact without losing the evidence of earlier work.

The Department's task is not to erase uncertainty from its records. It is to make uncertainty visible, manageable, and open to investigation.

6.9. A Working Guide to Scientific Names

  • Write the complete binomial at first mention. The genus begins with a capital letter, the specific epithet with a lowercase letter, and both words are italicised.
  • Check the spelling against a reliable taxonomic source. Do not assume that an old label or copied catalogue entry is correct.
  • Separate the name from the identification. A correctly written name can still have been applied to the wrong specimen.
  • Record the source of the name. Where possible, include the author, publication, date, and relevant type information.
  • Preserve verified synonyms. Earlier names may be essential for locating historical records.
  • Distinguish established names from provisional labels. A field designation is not automatically a formally published name.
  • Document uncertainty honestly. If a specimen cannot be identified with confidence, state the limitation rather than supplying a name for the sake of completeness.
  • Revisit old determinations when new evidence becomes available. An identification made decades ago may remain correct, but its age alone is not evidence that it is correct.

These principles are simple enough to apply at a field station and important enough to govern the largest botanical archive in the world. A name should lead the reader towards evidence. If it conceals the evidence, discourages further examination, or makes an uncertain claim appear settled, it has been used badly.

Closing Remarks

During my early years at the Grand Library, I once spent several afternoons searching for a plant mentioned in a physician's notebook. The entry described a remedy prepared from the roots of a small woodland species, but the name used by the physician did not appear in any of our current catalogues.

Eventually, I found the name in a much older herbarium register. It had been crossed out, replaced by another, and then copied incorrectly into a later index. The plant had not disappeared. Its name had simply travelled through the records less carefully than the specimens themselves.

I remember the incident because it was an ordinary failure, not an extraordinary one. No expedition had been lost, no rare species destroyed, and no great scientific controversy resolved. Yet an observation that might have been useful to later researchers had become difficult to recover through a chain of small errors.

This is the work that nomenclature asks of us: attention to details that may seem insignificant until someone needs them.

Scientific names are among the most useful instruments in botany. They allow knowledge to travel beyond the boundaries of a language, a province, or a lifetime. But they remain instruments, and their value depends upon the care with which we use them.

A name is not the plant. It is a shared means of referring to it, locating its records and examining the evidence associated with it. For that purpose, a name must be precise enough to support research, yet open to correction when the evidence demands it.

A change of name must not erase the history of a specimen or the observations attached to it. Equally, a familiar name should never be treated as proof that an identification is beyond question. Names help us organise evidence; they do not replace it.

This is the principle by which the Department of Botany maintains its register. Every scientific name in this volume should be read as a carefully supported identification, not as a claim that the living plant has been fully understood.

6. Plant Morphology Applied to Identification

7.6. Preparing a Taxonomic Plate

For a taxonomic plate, I recommend including the following elements where relevant:

  • A complete view showing the overall growth habit.
  • A detailed leaf, including its attachment to the stem.
  • A close view of the flower, with its important structures visible.
  • A fruit or seed, if available.
  • A detail of any feature that distinguishes the specimen from related species.
  • A scale reference and the specimen's catalogue number.

Not every species requires a crowded plate. A simple plant with few diagnostic features may be represented clearly in one main view and two details. A complex flower may require a separate dissection drawing. The purpose is to make identification easier, not to fill every available space.

The illustrator must also resist the temptation to improve the plant. A slightly irregular leaf should not be made symmetrical for aesthetic reasons if its irregularity is real. A faded flower should not be restored to a brighter colour without indicating that the colour is based on another observation. The plate should be beautiful where possible, but fidelity comes first.

In the Grand Library, illustrations are treated as part of the scientific record. Each is linked to a specimen or a documented observation wherever possible. The artist's name is recorded alongside the botanical author, and any uncertainty about the source material is noted.

This practice recognises that scientific illustration is a form of observation in its own right. The illustrator may notice a detail that escaped the collector's attention. A carefully drawn branching pattern, for instance, can reveal an arrangement that was difficult to recognise in the field. Such discoveries should be recorded and checked rather than dismissed as artistic interpretation.

The best plate does more than show a plant. It teaches the reader what to look at.

Closing Remarks

When I began working in the Department of Botany, I believed that experience would eventually make identification straightforward. After enough years, I thought, the important differences would become obvious.

Experience does improve the eye. It teaches the observer where to look, which structures deserve attention, and which similarities are likely to be misleading. It also reveals how much can be missed when a specimen is examined too quickly.

I now spend more time on certain plants than I did when I was younger. A familiar leaf may belong to an unfamiliar species. A flower that appears ordinary may contain a structure worth examining under magnification. A plant collected without its roots may require another expedition before its identity can be settled.

There is no shame in returning to a specimen. The shame lies in pretending that the first examination was complete when the evidence says otherwise.

Morphology gives us a language for describing the visible structure of plants. It allows observations made by different people, in different places and at different times, to be compared with care. It does not eliminate uncertainty, but it gives us a way to investigate it.

When you next encounter an unfamiliar plant, do not begin by asking what it is called. Begin with the structures before you. Look at the stem, follow the arrangement of the leaves, examine the buds, and wait for the flower or fruit if those structures are needed. Record the details that distinguish this individual from the plants around it.

A name may come later.

First learn to see the plant. Then learn how to describe it. Only after that should you decide what to call it.

7. Methods of Observation and Description

On recording what is seen, what is measured, and what remains unknown.

8.1. The Discipline of Observation

A botanical expedition does not begin when a specimen is placed between sheets of paper. It begins when the observer first encounters a plant and decides to pay attention.

Many promising records are weakened by a simple habit: the collector notices something interesting, forms an explanation, and records the explanation instead of the observation.

A flower closes when a shadow passes over it. One researcher writes that it fears the dark. Another records that the petals closed at 16:40, approximately three minutes after the light was obstructed. The first statement suggests a cause that has not been established. The second provides information that another researcher can test.

The difference is fundamental. Botanical observation requires patience, consistency, and a willingness to leave questions unanswered. The investigator must distinguish what was directly seen from what was inferred, what was reported by another person, and what remains speculation.

This does not mean that interpretation should be avoided. Science advances by proposing explanations. But an explanation is most useful when the observations supporting it have been recorded clearly enough for someone else to examine them.

The Department of Botany therefore teaches field researchers to maintain two related records: an observational account, containing the details of the encounter, and an interpretative account, containing provisional identifications, explanations, and questions for further investigation. The two need not be kept in separate books, but they must remain distinguishable.

A sentence such as “the leaves became pale after three days of rain” records a sequence of events. “The plant loses colour because of excess moisture” proposes a cause. The second may be correct, but it requires additional evidence.

In the Known Realms, where unusual plant behaviour is sometimes attributed to magic, this distinction becomes especially important. A phenomenon may be extraordinary without its explanation being extraordinary. The investigator must first establish what happened, under which conditions, and whether it can be observed again.

The best field researchers are not those who arrive with an answer for every question. They are those who know which questions their evidence can answer.

8.2. Preparing for Fieldwork

Good observations depend partly on preparation. A botanist who arrives at a site without suitable equipment may be forced to estimate measurements, omit important details, or rely on memory after returning to the Library.

The equipment required varies according to the purpose of the expedition. A survey of flowering plants in an accessible meadow may require little more than a notebook, a hand lens, a ruler, and a camera. An expedition into a remote mountain range may need maps, navigation instruments, weather protection, specimen containers, and equipment for preserving material.

A basic botanical field kit may include:

  • A durable field notebook and waterproof writing materials.
  • Pencils, a ruler, and a measuring tape.
  • A hand lens for examining small structures.
  • A camera or another means of recording images.
  • Labels and unique collection numbers.
  • Maps and suitable navigation equipment.
  • Sample envelopes, bags, or containers appropriate to the material.
  • A plant press when collecting is permitted and necessary.
  • Protective equipment appropriate to the environment and the plants being studied.

A field kit should be adapted to the work rather than assembled for appearance. Heavy equipment is of little use if it discourages the researcher from carrying the notebook that contains the essential observations.

Before departure, the investigator should establish the purpose of the survey. Is the aim to document the distribution of a known species, compare populations, identify an unknown plant, or record seasonal changes? The answer determines what information needs to be collected.

An expedition studying the flowering period of a woodland species will require repeated observations across the season. A survey of an unfamiliar marsh plant may prioritise detailed photographs, measurements, habitat notes, and specimens showing reproductive structures.

Preparation also includes checking whether collection is permitted. Rare plants, protected habitats, privately managed land, and sensitive ecosystems may require permission or non-destructive methods. A scientific investigation should not damage the population it seeks to understand.

In the Grand Library, an expedition plan includes a brief statement of purpose, the intended locations, the equipment required, the proposed methods, and the procedures for recording specimens. The plan may change when conditions demand it, but any significant change should be documented.

Fieldwork rarely proceeds exactly as expected. That is not a reason to abandon method. It is a reason to record what changed.

8.3. The Field Notebook

The field notebook is one of the most important tools in botanical research. A specimen can be preserved for centuries, but many details that made it interesting may disappear within hours of collection.

Flower colour may fade during drying. A scent may vanish. A leaf may lose its natural position. A plant that grew in standing water may be pressed flat and stored in a cabinet without any visible indication of its original surroundings. The notebook preserves these observations.

Each specimen or observation should be linked to a unique identifier. The number must be recorded in the notebook and on the associated specimen label, photograph, or container. It should not be reused for a different collection.

A field record normally includes the following information:

Collection number

A unique identifier for the collection or observation.

Collector

Name of the person responsible for the record.

Date and time

When the observation or collection took place.

Locality

A precise description of the place, with coordinates when available.

Habitat

Soil, moisture, light exposure, terrain, and surrounding vegetation.

Plant description

Growth habit, measurements, leaves, flowers, fruits, and other relevant features.

Developmental stage

Budding, flowering, fruiting, dormant, or another observed stage.

Associated observations

Pollinators, neighbouring plants, damage, scent, colour changes, or unusual behaviour.

Identification

Current identification, confidence level, and unresolved questions.

Not every field is relevant to every specimen, and the absence of information should not be concealed. If the roots were not examined, write that they were not examined. If the exact collection time is unknown, record the uncertainty rather than inventing a value.

The notebook should be written as close to the time of observation as possible. Notes reconstructed several days later tend to lose details and merge separate events into a single remembered impression.

A useful entry is specific enough that another botanist could understand the circumstances without having accompanied the expedition. “Small plant near stream” is a poor locality description. “Approximately twelve paces east of the old stone footbridge, growing in saturated soil beneath the northern bank” is more useful, provided the landmarks and measurements are recorded accurately.

Even this description should be supplemented with a map reference or coordinates when available. A footbridge may collapse, a path may be renamed, and a familiar landmark may no longer exist.

The notebook itself should be preserved after the expedition. It is part of the scientific record, not a temporary aid to memory. Later researchers may discover that a minor observation in the margin is relevant to a question the original collector never considered.

8.4. Recording Measurements

Measurements make descriptions more precise and allow comparisons between specimens. They are particularly valuable when differences in size, proportions, or development may help distinguish closely related species.

The simplest measurements include plant height, stem diameter, leaf length and width, petiole length, flower diameter, and fruit dimensions. Larger specimens may require measurements of trunk circumference, canopy spread, or the distance between neighbouring individuals.

Measurements should be taken using a consistent method. A leaf measured from the base of its blade to the tip is not directly comparable with one measured including its petiole unless the distinction is recorded. Likewise, plant height should specify whether the measurement was taken from the ground to the highest living point, the tip of an inflorescence, or another reference point.

The Department uses the metric system for ordinary botanical measurements, with millimetres for small structures, centimetres for many leaves and flowers, and metres for larger plants and habitat features.

Precision should reflect the instrument and the purpose of the work. Recording a leaf as 47.3 millimetres long may be appropriate when using a suitable measuring tool. Recording a tree as 18.472 metres tall from a rough visual estimate creates an appearance of accuracy that the method does not support.

This is false precision. A measurement is not more reliable simply because it contains more digits.

When a feature varies across one plant, measure several representative examples rather than choosing a single convenient value. For a study comparing populations, the number of individuals and the method of selecting them should also be recorded. A sample taken only from the largest plants may not represent the population as a whole.

Measurements can be affected by the condition of the specimen. A leaf may curl as it dries, a fruit may shrink, and a flexible stem may bend under its own weight. If the measurement was taken from dried material rather than a living plant, that fact should be stated.

The same principle applies to observations of unusual plants. If a stem appears to lengthen rapidly overnight, record its length at defined intervals, the method used, and the conditions under which the measurements were taken. A dramatic result is worth investigating, but it should not be accepted merely because it is dramatic.

8.5. Colour, Scent, Texture, and Other Changing Features

Some of the most useful botanical characters are also the most difficult to preserve.

Colour is a familiar example. A flower may appear white in strong sunlight and cream-coloured in shade. A red pigment may fade rapidly after collection. The same leaf may look different when wet, dry, backlit, or viewed against dark soil.

Colour descriptions should therefore be made under reasonably consistent conditions whenever possible. If a precise colour reference system is available, it can improve consistency between observers. Otherwise, a clear written description and a photograph taken under recorded conditions may be preferable to an elaborate but subjective name.

Photographs are useful, but they are not perfect records of colour. Lighting, exposure, display settings, and the properties of the camera can alter the appearance of a specimen. A photograph should complement the written observation rather than replace it.

Scent requires similar care. Record whether it was perceived from the intact plant, a crushed leaf, a cut stem, or a flower. These are different observations. A scent released by crushing a leaf should not be described as though it were naturally present in the surrounding air.

The observer should also note its intensity and the conditions under which it was detected. Terms such as faint, strong, sweet, resinous, and earthy can be useful, but they depend partly on perception. Where several researchers are involved, a shared descriptive vocabulary can help.

Texture is often overlooked in written accounts. A leaf may be leathery, papery, fleshy, brittle, velvety, or rough. The stem may be smooth, ridged, or covered in hairs. These details can be recorded directly, provided the plant can be handled safely.

The same caution applies to sap, latex, and other secretions. Their colour, consistency, and apparent behaviour may be diagnostic, but unfamiliar substances should not be tasted or handled without appropriate precautions. A botanical investigation does not require unnecessary exposure to potentially irritating, toxic, or otherwise hazardous material.

For the purposes of the Grand Library, unusual sensory properties are recorded in plain language before any interpretation is attempted.

At 18:10, the upper surface of three mature leaves appeared pale blue when viewed in natural twilight. The colour was less apparent under a covered lantern. No visible colour change was recorded in the same leaves at midday.

This is a useful observation even if its cause remains unknown. It identifies the time, the structures involved, and a condition under which the effect appeared to change. A later researcher can attempt to reproduce the observation.

By contrast, the statement “the plant awakens at dusk” introduces a metaphor that may be evocative but does not tell us exactly what happened. Metaphors have their place in poetry. A field notebook should make room for the evidence first.

8.6. Describing the Habitat

A plant does not grow independently of its surroundings. Soil, water availability, light, temperature, terrain, neighbouring organisms, and disturbance can all influence its growth and distribution.

Habitat information is therefore an essential part of a botanical record. It may help explain why a species occurs in one locality and not another, why its appearance varies between populations, or why a previously documented plant can no longer be found at a site.

The description should begin with the physical environment. Record whether the plant grows in woodland, open grassland, marsh, exposed rock, cultivated ground, a riverbank, or another identifiable habitat. Note the soil's apparent texture and moisture, the degree of shade, the slope, and any relevant water features.

Where measurements are possible, use them. “Growing in a shallow pool” is useful; “growing in approximately five centimetres of standing water at the edge of a shallow pool” is more precise, provided the depth was actually measured.

The surrounding vegetation is also important. Record the most relevant neighbouring species where they can be identified, particularly when the plant appears to be associated with a particular community. A plant found beneath the same tree species at several sites may warrant further investigation, but a single association does not establish dependence.

The observer should distinguish between a plant's immediate location and the broader landscape. A specimen may grow in a patch of moss within a woodland, on a north-facing slope above a river valley, in a region characterised by seasonal rainfall. Each scale can provide useful information.

Environmental conditions should be recorded at the time of observation rather than inferred solely from the appearance of the plant. A dry-looking leaf does not prove that the soil was dry, and a plant growing near water may have roots extending into a different moisture regime.

For the Known Realms, this distinction is particularly important in locations affected by unusual environmental phenomena. A plant growing near a magical spring should be documented with the same ordinary habitat information as any other specimen. The spring's presence is a relevant observation; its supposed influence is a separate question.

If several plants in the same area show a distinctive feature, record how many were examined and whether the feature occurred in every individual. The phrase “all plants in the marsh glow at night” should be used only if the observation genuinely supports such a broad claim. If the researcher examined six plants along one bank, the record should say so.

A habitat description is not complete because it is lengthy. It is complete enough when it captures the conditions relevant to the question being investigated.

8.7. Repeated Observations and Seasonal Change

A single visit provides a snapshot. Repeated observations reveal patterns.

Many plants change considerably throughout the year. Buds develop into flowers, flowers produce fruits, leaves emerge and fall, and dormant structures persist underground while the visible plant disappears. Some species respond rapidly to rainfall or temperature, while others show changes only over several seasons.

A botanist investigating these patterns should return to the same individuals or population at planned intervals whenever possible. The dates and conditions of each visit should be recorded so that the observations can be compared.

Consider a hypothetical plant in the western highlands that appears to flower only after heavy rain. Three observations made after separate storms may suggest an association, but they do not necessarily establish that rainfall causes flowering. The plants may also respond to temperature, day length, soil conditions, or a combination of factors.

A careful record might include the date of the first visible buds, the date of flowering, recent rainfall, air temperature, and the condition of neighbouring plants. Repeated observations over several seasons would provide a stronger basis for identifying a consistent pattern.

Photographs taken from similar angles can be especially helpful. They may show changes in leaf size, stem height, flowering stage, or the extent of a plant colony. A fixed observation point also makes it easier to detect changes in the surrounding habitat.

When studying an unusual plant, the same approach applies to any reported behaviour. If a flower appears to close when approached, observe it at different distances and times, record the behaviour of several flowers, and note possible alternative explanations such as changes in light, temperature, or movement of the observer.

The purpose is not to dismiss unusual phenomena. It is to determine what, precisely, makes them unusual.

The Department maintains long-term observation records for selected plants in its living collections. These records have proved useful not only for taxonomy but also for horticulture and conservation. They reveal when a species is likely to flower, how its growth responds to different conditions, and which features remain consistent across years.

A single unusual season may produce an interesting entry. A sequence of observations may change our understanding of a species.

8.8. Comparing Individuals and Populations

A botanical description should account for variation. No two living plants are necessarily identical, even when they belong to the same species and grow side by side.

Some differences reflect age. Others result from environmental conditions, damage, disease, or ordinary genetic variation. A population may contain individuals with slightly different leaf sizes, flower colours, or growth habits without representing several separate species.

When comparing individuals, the researcher should identify which characters remain consistent and which vary. The range of variation may be as informative as the average.

For example, suppose a population of marsh plants has leaves measuring between four and eight centimetres in length. Recording only a single leaf measuring six centimetres would conceal much of the observed variation. A more useful description provides the range, the number of leaves measured, and, where appropriate, a measure of central tendency.

The same applies to colour, height, and flowering time. If the researcher records only the earliest-flowering individuals, the population may appear to have a shorter flowering season than it actually does.

Sampling should be planned with the research question in mind. If the aim is to describe a population, the researcher should avoid selecting only the largest, healthiest, or most visually striking specimens. If the aim is to investigate an unusual form, the unusual individuals should be compared with ordinary ones from the same locality and, where possible, with populations elsewhere.

This is also where negative observations become useful. If a distinctive feature is absent in most individuals, that absence should be recorded. A pale flower occurring in one plant among a hundred is a different finding from a population in which every flower is pale.

The Grand Library encourages researchers to preserve examples of variation in their records rather than forcing every specimen to conform to a single idealised illustration.

A species description should represent the plant as it occurs, not as the author wishes it to appear.

8.9. Drawing and Photographing Observations

Written notes are indispensable, but some structures are easier to understand visually. A drawing or photograph can preserve the position of leaves, the shape of a flower, the pattern of branching, or the relationship between a plant and its habitat.

Photographs should be taken with a purpose. A general image can show the growth habit and surroundings, while close views can document leaves, buds, flowers, fruits, and diagnostic details. Including a ruler or another suitable scale reference helps communicate size, provided the reference is positioned correctly.

The observer should record which photographs belong to which collection number. Images without identifiers may become difficult to associate with the correct specimen, particularly after an expedition has produced hundreds of files.

Where possible, photographs should show both the overall plant and the details needed for identification. A beautiful image of a flower may be of limited scientific value if it conceals the leaf arrangement or the structure of the stem.

Drawings offer different advantages. The illustrator can isolate a diagnostic feature, show an internal structure, or compare several stages of development on one plate. A well-prepared botanical illustration may communicate the form of a plant more clearly than a photograph taken under poor conditions.

Nevertheless, drawings must not silently combine observations from different specimens. If the flower comes from one individual and the leaves from another, the plate should indicate that fact where it matters. A composite illustration can be useful, but its construction must be transparent.

The same applies to reconstruction. A damaged specimen may lack a petal or a portion of its stem. The missing part can be shown in a separate explanatory drawing, but it should not be presented as though it were preserved in the original material.

For an illustrator working with the Department, the first task is to understand the specimen. The second is to decide which details the reader needs to see. Composition, line weight, and visual clarity should serve those purposes.

The most successful scientific illustration is not necessarily the most elaborate. It is the one that allows another person to observe the relevant features with greater accuracy.

8.10. When the Plant Cannot Be Collected

Collection is not always possible, and it is not always desirable.

A plant may be rare, protected, difficult to reach, or growing in a location where disturbance would damage the population. It may also be too large to preserve as a complete specimen. In other cases, the collector may lack the necessary permits or equipment.

Non-destructive documentation can still produce valuable evidence. Detailed photographs, measurements, sketches, habitat descriptions, and repeated observations may support a useful provisional identification. Where appropriate, fallen material or naturally shed leaves may provide additional information without harming the plant.

The limitations must be stated clearly. A photograph may show the colour and outline of a flower but fail to reveal its internal structure. A tree observed from the ground may conceal reproductive features high in the canopy. A plant that cannot be revisited may remain difficult to identify beyond genus level.

Such limitations do not invalidate the record. They define the questions that remain open.

The Department's archives include early accounts of plants that were never collected. Some describe species from remote islands or unstable mountain slopes; others record encounters with plants that could not be approached safely. These accounts are valuable, but their evidential status differs from that of a specimen that can be re-examined.

A responsible catalogue makes that distinction visible. It does not treat a detailed eyewitness account as equivalent to preserved material, nor does it discard the account merely because a specimen is missing.

In the Known Realms, several unusual plants are reported to withdraw beneath the soil when disturbed. Some accounts are credible enough to justify further study; others cannot be verified. Researchers investigating these reports should document the circumstances, seek independent observations, and avoid disturbing the habitat in an attempt to force a response.

An unknown plant does not owe the investigator a specimen. Sometimes the most responsible outcome of an expedition is a carefully documented observation and a recommendation to return under better conditions.

8.11. A Model Field Record

The following record demonstrates how the principles in this chapter can be combined. The locality and species are invented for this volume, but the format follows established botanical field-recording practices.

Grand Library · Department of Botany

Field Record EM-017

Vitraluma palustris — provisional identification

Collection details

Locality: Eastern Marshes, lower fen district; shallow drainage channel approximately half a mile east of the old fen road, on the northern margin of the lower marsh. Exact coordinates are recorded in the expedition map.

Date and time: 18th day of the Rainmonth, 09:20–11:45.

Observer: Professor Elowen Thorne, accompanied by two field assistants.

Weather: Overcast, light wind, intermittent drizzle. Air temperature measured at 12°C.

Habitat: Saturated dark soil beside shallow standing water. Partial shade from low shrubs; mosses and several unidentified sedges present nearby.

Description of the plant

General appearance: Small herbaceous plants growing in loose groups. Most flowering stems erect; several peripheral shoots leaning towards open ground.

Leaves: Simple, narrow-lanceolate, with smooth margins. Alternate arrangement confirmed on six flowering stems. Leaf lengths recorded individually in the notebook.

Flowers: Pale blue in natural daylight. Single flowers borne near the ends of flowering shoots. Internal floral structures not fully examined during the first visit.

Fruits: None observed.

Observations and interpretation

Additional observations: The upper leaf margins appeared translucent under overcast light. No visible luminescence was observed during the morning survey. Two earlier reports describe a faint blue glow after dusk; these reports remain unverified by the present expedition.

Collection status: Photographs and a small sample collected under expedition authorisation. The majority of the observed population was left undisturbed.

Preliminary conclusion: The combination of leaf arrangement, leaf shape, growth habit, and floral appearance is consistent with the provisional identification. A return visit during fruit development is recommended.

Questions for the next visit: Examine the internal floral structures; document fruit and seed morphology; repeat observations of leaf translucency under different lighting conditions; investigate the reported nocturnal glow without assuming its cause.

The value of this record lies not in its length but in its organisation. Another botanist can identify what was examined, what was measured, what was reported by others, and what remains to be done.

If the provisional identification proves incorrect, the observations will still be useful. The leaf arrangement, habitat, measurements, and photographs do not become meaningless because the name changes. A revised conclusion need not destroy the evidence on which the earlier conclusion was based.

8.12. Errors That Weaken a Field Record

Even experienced researchers make mistakes. The purpose of a consistent method is not to eliminate every error but to make errors easier to recognise and correct.

The most common problems include:

  • Relying on memory. Important details are recorded hours or days after the observation, when colour, measurements, and circumstances may already have been forgotten.
  • Using vague descriptions. Words such as “large”, “nearby”, and “unusual” are not always useful without a reference point or explanation.
  • Confusing observation with interpretation. A plant that changes colour is recorded as responding to magic before other explanations have been considered.
  • Omitting collection identifiers. Photographs, notes, and specimens become separated, making it difficult to establish which records belong together.
  • Recording only exceptional features. The researcher documents the glowing flower but neglects the leaves, stem, habitat, and reproductive structures needed for identification.
  • Ignoring variation. One individual is treated as representative of an entire population without further examination.
  • Claiming unsupported precision. Estimated values are written as though they came from exact measurements.
  • Failing to record uncertainty. Missing structures, uncertain localities, and incomplete observations are concealed rather than acknowledged.

One further mistake deserves special attention: altering an original record without preserving the correction history.

A notebook may contain a misidentified plant, an incorrect measurement, or a locality later shown to be wrong. Corrections are necessary, but the original entry should remain legible whenever possible, with the amendment dated and explained. This allows later researchers to understand how the record developed.

The same principle applies to digital catalogues. Corrections should be traceable, and changes to an identification should not erase the fact that an earlier researcher reached a different conclusion.

The scientific record is strengthened by transparent correction. It is weakened by the appearance that no mistake was ever made.

Closing Remarks

A field notebook is a record of attention.

It preserves the details that seemed worth noticing at a particular moment, often before the observer understands their importance. A measurement taken beside a marsh may later help distinguish two species. A note about the direction of a slope may explain why a population flowers earlier than another. An observation of a faint scent may become significant when a related plant is examined years later.

None of these possibilities can be guaranteed. That is why the record must be made carefully in the first place.

I have returned to old notebooks many times, sometimes to confirm an identification and sometimes to discover that I had overlooked a detail. There are entries in my own hand that I would now describe differently. Some contain measurements that should have been repeated. Others record conclusions that I would hesitate to defend today.

I have not removed them.

A scientific notebook should show what the observer knew, what the observer believed, and what the evidence allowed at the time. Later work may improve the interpretation, but it should not pretend that the earlier uncertainty never existed.

This is particularly important when studying plants that appear to behave in ways we cannot yet explain. The unknown is not a blank space to be filled with the first available story. It is a subject of investigation, and investigation requires a record that remains useful even when our first explanation fails.

The botanist's task is not simply to collect plants or describe their appearance. It is to create observations that can survive beyond the circumstances in which they were made.

Write down what happened. Record how you know it happened. Leave room for what you have not yet understood.

8. Reference Specimens and Herbaria

On the preservation of botanical evidence, the care of collections, and the quiet responsibility of keeping a plant available to those who have not yet learned its name.

9.1. What Is a Herbarium?

A herbarium is a collection of preserved plant specimens assembled for study, comparison, and reference. It may begin with a few pressed plants between sheets of paper; in a major institution, it becomes an organised archive of botanical evidence gathered over generations.

The Grand Library's Central Herbarium occupies three interconnected halls on the eastern side of the Department of Botany. Its oldest specimens predate the department itself. They were collected by physicians, royal gardeners, travelling scholars, agricultural surveyors, and people whose names appear nowhere beyond their field notebooks.

The collection is not arranged according to beauty or fame. A rare orchid may rest beside a modest grass, and a celebrated botanist's specimen may be stored several cabinets away from one gathered by an unknown apprentice. Their positions follow the catalogue system. A herbarium is not a gallery of curiosities; it is a working scientific resource.

Preserved specimens allow researchers to revisit earlier identifications, compare plants from different regions, investigate changes in morphology, and document a species' former distribution. A specimen gathered for a study of medicinal plants may later prove essential to the description of an unrecognised species. Its value depends on preservation and on the information that accompanies it.

A plant without a reliable record is a plant whose history may be impossible to recover.

9.2. The Specimen as Evidence

A description tells us what an observer recorded; an illustration represents appearance; a field notebook preserves circumstances and observations. A specimen offers something different: physical material that another researcher can examine independently.

A specimen collected to support a scientific study is commonly called a voucher specimen. It provides a physical reference for the observations or conclusions associated with it. In the Grand Library, voucher specimens are especially important when documenting medicinal plants, newly recorded populations, unusual ecological relationships, and uncertain identifications.

The presence of a specimen does not guarantee that a conclusion is correct. The plant may have been misidentified, the label may contain an error, or the material may not represent the full variation of a species. A well-documented specimen nevertheless makes mistakes easier to recognise and correct. Without physical evidence, a disputed identification can become a contest between authorities; with evidence, the disagreement can be returned to the plant itself.

A specimen represents an individual or a collection event, not a species in every circumstance. It cannot establish by itself how common a plant is, how long it lives, or how its appearance changes through a season. Those questions require additional specimens, field observations, experiments, and other evidence.

9.3. From the Field to the Collection

Preparation begins before a plant reaches the laboratory. The collector must consider whether collection is justified, whether photographs and non-destructive observations will suffice, and whether permission is required. When collection is appropriate, the material should preserve as many useful diagnostic features as possible: a flowering branch may be more informative than a handful of leaves, while the roots of a small herb may be essential to identification.

The collector records the specimen number, locality, date, habitat, and relevant observations before these details are lost. Each gathering receives a unique identifier that follows the material through pressing, drying, identification, mounting, and storage.

The preparators of the Grand Library follow a rule that seems obvious until it is broken: never allow an unlabelled specimen to become separated from the record that identifies it. A beautiful specimen without a reliable locality or collection history may remain useful for morphological study, but it is far less useful for understanding distribution or ecology.

9.4. Pressing and Drying

For many vascular plants, the familiar preservation method is pressing followed by drying. The plant is arranged between absorbent papers, with layers that encourage air circulation, then held under enough pressure to flatten it without crushing diagnostic structures.

Leaves are spread where possible; flowers are positioned so their important features remain visible; and overlapping parts are adjusted to reveal useful characters. If the specimen is too large for one sheet, portions may be folded carefully or divided across several sheets, provided their relationship is documented.

Drying must be sufficiently rapid to limit mould and deterioration. Once dry, the specimen may be mounted on durable archival paper and secured with suitable adhesive, linen strips, or thread. Detached seeds or fragments may be kept in an attached packet. Labels, paper, adhesives, and inks must be chosen with long-term stability in mind.

Not every plant suits this method. Succulent tissues may collapse, fleshy fruits can be difficult to press, and delicate flowers may lose important structures. Dried fragments, separately preserved fruits, fluid-preserved material, microscopic preparations, or photographs may be used alongside the main specimen. No single technique answers every botanical question.

9.5. The Label: A Second Specimen

A herbarium sheet is incomplete without its label. Experienced botanists often read the label before examining the plant, because it establishes the circumstances in which the specimen was collected.

Identity

The name used when collected or identified; later determinations are recorded separately.

Collector and number

The person who gathered the material and the number assigned to that gathering.

Date and locality

When and where it was collected, described precisely enough to distinguish the site.

Habitat and observations

Growing conditions and features that may disappear during preservation, such as colour, scent, or growth habit.

Institutional record

The herbarium holding the specimen and the identifier used to retrieve its catalogue entry.

Historical labels may give only a region, a month, and a name no longer in use. A curator may infer a likely locality from a collector's journal or historical maps, but the reconstruction must be recorded as an inference rather than silently added to the original label.

At the Grand Library, the original label is never replaced merely because its information has become inconvenient. Corrections and later identifications are attached as separate annotations. A label is not a decorative caption; it is the record that allows the plant to speak beyond the place and time in which it was gathered.

9.6. Accession Numbers and the Collection Register

Every specimen accepted into the permanent collection receives an accession record linking the physical material to its collection history, preparation method, current identification, storage location, and any restrictions on access.

A collector's number identifies a gathering within the collector's own sequence. An institutional accession number links the material to the receiving herbarium's records. One gathering may produce duplicate specimens deposited in separate institutions; each is managed independently, while the records preserve their shared origin.

Grand Library · Central Herbarium

Accession GLH-1842-0671

Current determination: Vitraluma palustris, Glass-marsh lanternflower

Collector's number: E. Thorne 214

Locality: Eastern Glass Marsh, lower reed terraces

Collection date: 18th day of the Rainmoot, Year 612

Record history: Originally identified provisionally as Luma palustris; revised after comparative examination.

Associated materials: Two pressed flowering stems, one fruit packet, field notebook entry, and three habitat illustrations.

Status: Identification accepted provisionally; reproductive material requires further comparison.

The current name does not erase earlier names. Recording previous determinations allows researchers to discover how a specimen has been interpreted and why those interpretations changed. The register must also make the specimen physically locatable. If the catalogue identifies an item that no one can find, the collection has failed in one of its most basic duties.

9.7. How a Herbarium Is Organised

There is no single arrangement suitable for every herbarium. The Grand Library uses a primarily taxonomic arrangement for its main collection, while historical holdings, regional materials, and specimens requiring special conditions have additional catalogue distinctions.

General Herbarium

The main collection of dried and mounted vascular plants, used for routine identification and comparison.

Historical Herbarium

Early collections, original field materials, and specimens associated with significant expeditions.

Cryptogamic Collection

Mosses, liverworts, ferns, and other groups that may require specialised preparation or storage.

Fluid-Preserved Collection

Selected flowers, fruits, and other plant material stored in suitable preservative solutions.

Reference Collection

Frequently consulted material for teaching and routine comparisons, reducing handling of fragile historical specimens.

The boundaries are practical rather than absolute. A tree may be documented through a pressed branch, bark, seeds, photographs, and a wood specimen. These components must remain linked in the records even when their storage requirements differ. A well-organised herbarium is a system that connects an object with its history and the knowledge accumulated around it.

9.8. Conservation: Keeping the Collection Usable

Preservation does not end when a specimen has dried and been mounted. Paper becomes brittle, adhesives fail, labels detach, insects damage plant material, and changes in temperature or humidity accelerate deterioration. Cabinets must be inspected, environmental conditions monitored, and specimens examined for damage.

A dried plant may look robust, yet leaves and flowers can fracture under pressure. Researchers are trained to support sheets properly, avoid touching fragile parts, and return folders to their correct positions. A loose fragment should not be discarded merely because its original position is unclear; it may belong in an attached packet or require investigation.

Historical materials demand restraint. Replacing an old sheet with fresh paper may improve its appearance but remove evidence of handling or separate the plant from earlier annotations. The Grand Library follows a principle established after an unfortunate incident in its western cabinets: nothing is repaired merely to make it look new. The aim is to preserve the specimen, its records, and its usefulness.

Pest management relies on prevention, inspection, and appropriate treatment. Suspect specimens are isolated and assessed rather than returned immediately to general storage. When conservation succeeds, its work is often invisible: a specimen remains available for decades or centuries without drawing attention to the conditions that made its survival possible.

9.9. Ethical Collection and the Limits of Ownership

The existence of a herbarium does not justify collecting every plant that interests a botanist. Some species are rare, geographically restricted, or vulnerable to disturbance. Others grow in protected habitats or on land where permission is required. Even when a plant is not formally protected, removing a substantial portion of a small population may be irresponsible.

Before collecting, researchers must consider whether permission is needed, whether the population can withstand removal, and whether the question can be answered through less damaging means. A photograph or detailed field description may suffice for some questions; for others, physical material is essential. The decision should follow the research need, not the collector's desire to possess an unusual specimen.

Botanical collections also have histories shaped by unequal access to land, knowledge, and authority. Some specimens were gathered without local consent or removed from places where collection was contested. A modern institution can document provenance, acknowledge contributors, review claims of ownership, and avoid repeating those practices. Where local knowledge is shared, its source and any conditions on its use should be recorded carefully.

A collection is only one part of conservation. Removing a plant from a threatened habitat may preserve evidence of its existence, but it does not preserve the living population or the relationships that sustain it. The best herbarium is not the one that has taken the most. It is the one that has preserved useful evidence with the least unnecessary harm.

9.10. Loans, Exchanges, and Shared Collections

No herbarium contains every plant a researcher may need. Institutions therefore share information and, under suitable conditions, lend specimens. A researcher studying a difficult genus may need material held in several countries, each with its own catalogue and handling requirements.

A formal loan identifies the material and purpose of the study and establishes responsibility for safe handling and return. Rare, fragile, or historically significant specimens may be excluded from physical loans; high-resolution images or supervised access may be offered instead.

Duplicates from a single gathering can be deposited in different institutions. Each becomes an independent physical reference, while the records preserve their common origin. Exchange must never become indiscriminate transfer: legality, conservation concerns, source institutions' rights, and restrictions on sensitive material remain relevant.

Every outgoing loan at the Grand Library is recorded, and each returned specimen is checked. Damage in transit is documented rather than concealed. The purpose is not to discourage research, but to ensure the same material remains available after the current study has ended. Knowledge grows through comparison; collections must be shared carefully enough that comparison remains possible.

9.11. Digitisation: The Collection Beyond the Cabinets

A specimen can be studied only by those who can access it, physically or through a reliable representation. Travel may be expensive or impossible, and fragile material cannot withstand repeated handling. Digitisation helps address both problems.

The Grand Library's programme produces high-resolution images linked to catalogue records. A useful digital specimen includes the complete sheet, label, accession number, annotations, and, where necessary, close views of small features. An image without reliable locality, collector, or specimen identity loses much of its scientific value.

Digital records allow comparisons across institutions and make patterns in leaf shape, flowering time, distribution, or past identifications easier to investigate. Yet an image cannot reveal every feature. It may not show leaf thickness, surface texture, internal fruit structure, or the arrangement of minute floral parts. Colour can also be affected by lighting, preservation, and imaging.

Digital records are access points to evidence, not proof that physical examination is unnecessary. The physical specimen remains the primary object; its digital representation makes the collection more accessible.

9.12. Living Collections and Seed Archives

A herbarium cannot preserve every aspect of a plant's life. Living collections allow researchers to observe growth, flowering, reproduction, and responses to environmental conditions. Seed collections may preserve material for future research, restoration, or propagation, although success depends on the species, seed viability, storage conditions, and quality of the records.

Other collections may include pollen, wood, DNA samples, preserved tissues, or cultures maintained under controlled conditions. Each captures a different kind of evidence. A pressed specimen may be linked to seeds from the same population, a living plant grown from a documented accession, and photographs taken during flowering.

These forms of evidence are complementary, not interchangeable. A cultivated plant may grow differently from one in its natural habitat; a seed sample may not preserve the population's full genetic diversity; and a pressed specimen cannot show how a plant responds to drought or how its flowers open during a day. A good institution understands what each collection can—and cannot—tell us.

9.13. The Glass-Marsh Specimen

Among the Grand Library's most frequently consulted records is a specimen of Vitraluma palustris, collected from the eastern Glass Marsh during the late winter of Year 612. Two flowering stems lie diagonally across the sheet, their leaves spread to reveal both surfaces. A small packet contains detached fruits. The label is faded at the edges, and a second annotation has been attached above it.

The original collector described the flowers as pale blue in daylight, becoming faintly luminous after dusk. This observation remains in the field record, not as an established property of the species. The mounted specimen cannot confirm the reported change, and no later study has reproduced it under controlled conditions.

For decades, the plant was catalogued provisionally as Luma palustris. A later examination of its floral structure suggested a distinct genus, and the determination was revised. The original name remains visible in the record history.

Comparisons with material from three other marshes raised further questions. Some plants attributed to Vitraluma palustris have narrower leaves; others produce fruits with a different number of chambers. These differences may represent ordinary variation, environmental effects, or more than one closely related species. The accession remains open for further study.

Textbooks often favour examples with clean conclusions: a plant is collected, examined, and correctly named. Such examples teach procedure, but they can misrepresent research. Sometimes the most useful specimen is the one that refuses to fit neatly into a category. The Glass-Marsh collection matters because different researchers can examine the same evidence, challenge earlier interpretations, and identify what should be studied next.

9.14. What a Preserved Plant Cannot Tell Us

A herbarium specimen records a plant at a particular moment. Colours may fade, petals collapse, and scents disappear. A plant that changes shape in response to light, temperature, or touch may be preserved in only one state. Relationships with the environment must often be reconstructed from field notes, photographs, and other observations.

Reports of unusual plants require the same care. If a plant was seen glowing at night, the collector should record the conditions, duration, apparent intensity, and whether the event was repeated. If a leaf moved when touched, that movement should be distinguished from wind, temperature, or the handling itself. A specimen may help establish identity, but it cannot prove every claim made about behaviour.

The herbarium preserves what we were able to bring back. It does not preserve everything we saw, and it cannot preserve what we failed to notice.

The remark is often quoted by students. The surrounding note is less elegant and more useful: missing information should be acknowledged in the record, not supplied later by imagination. A herbarium cannot promise that nothing will be forgotten. It can make surviving evidence traceable, interpretable, and open to examination.

9.15. Principles for the Care of Reference Specimens

Keep specimen and record together.

An unidentified sheet may remain valuable; an untraceable sheet loses much of its usefulness.

Preserve identification history.

Record revisions and disagreements rather than erasing earlier determinations.

Choose suitable methods.

A pressed sheet is not appropriate for every plant or research question.

Handle according to condition.

Fragile material requires care; uncertain repairs belong with trained collection staff.

Collect responsibly.

Scientific value does not excuse unnecessary harm to rare species or vulnerable habitats.

Share with accountability.

Loans, exchanges, and digital access must respect conditions and restrictions.

Record uncertainty honestly.

A provisional identification is better than unsupported certainty.

Keep collections useful.

Material must remain discoverable, interpretable, and accessible for further study.

Closing Remarks

The oldest specimens in the Grand Library are often the quietest objects in the department. Their leaves are brown, their flowers have lost their colour, and their labels are written in hands no living member of staff can recognise. They do not announce their importance.

Yet each records an encounter between a person and a plant. Someone found it, decided it was worth preserving, and made an effort to ensure that another person could examine it later. Sometimes that effort was careful and successful; sometimes the locality was vague, the specimen incomplete, or the name incorrect. Even then, the material may retain information its collector never understood.

The task of the herbarium is not to turn every specimen into a perfect record. It is to protect the evidence that survives, document its limits, and make it available for new questions. A collection is not the whole of nature. It is a record of our attempts to study nature, with all the gaps, revisions, and discoveries those attempts involve.

When I walk through the Central Herbarium, I do not think of the cabinets as a storehouse of finished knowledge. I think of them as a room full of unfinished conversations. A label disagrees with a later determination. A leaf suggests a relationship not yet investigated. A specimen collected in a vanished marsh raises questions about a landscape that no longer exists.

The work of classification begins with observation, but it does not end when a name has been written on a label. It continues whenever someone opens a cabinet, removes a sheet, and looks again.

9. Variation, Hybrids, and Disputed Species

On the difficulty of drawing boundaries around living things, the plants that cross them, and the disagreements that arise when the evidence permits more than one interpretation.

10.1. The Problem of the Typical Plant

Every student of botany eventually encounters a specimen that seems to contradict the textbook. Its leaves are broader than expected, its flowers the wrong colour, or its fruits unlike those in the published illustration. The student checks the label and consults the identification key again. Sometimes the plant has been misidentified. But not always.

A species is not a collection of identical individuals. Plants vary in size, shape, colour, flowering time, reproductive success, and countless other characteristics. Some differences are inherited; others arise from the conditions in which a plant grows. Many result from an interaction between the two. A specimen that appears to contradict the textbook may be perfectly ordinary for its population.

A typical specimen is useful for teaching and comparison, but it should never become an imaginary standard against which every living individual is judged. I once examined marsh plants sent to the Grand Library as a possible new species. They were larger than the existing descriptions suggested, with unusually dark leaves and flowers that opened late in the season. The collector had already proposed a name.

Locality records and a second population changed the interpretation. The plants grew in unusually deep, nutrient-rich water; specimens from shallower ground displayed the narrower leaves and smaller flowers described in the literature. The proposed name was never published. The collector had observed genuine differences. The mistake was assuming that a difference necessarily indicated a taxonomic boundary.

Variation is evidence. It is not, by itself, proof of a new species.

10.2. Variation Within a Species

Variation occurs at several levels. Before deciding how to interpret it, the taxonomist must determine which level is relevant.

Individual variation

Individuals growing under similar conditions may differ. One produces more branches; another has slightly larger flowers or more leaves. Such differences may reflect inherited characteristics, developmental history, random variation, or environmental influences too subtle to measure. An individual difference rarely justifies a separate taxon, but it should be documented when a supposedly constant character proves less reliable than expected.

Environmental variation

Light affects growth and leaf development. Water stress can alter leaf size and thickness; nutrients may influence height, branching, and flowering. Wind-exposed plants may grow compactly, while shaded plants develop longer stems. A species can therefore look different in woodland, on a hillside, and in a marsh without the populations being taxonomically distinct. Environment must not become a convenient excuse for ignoring unusual features: the explanation needs evidence.

Developmental variation

Plants change as they mature. Seedlings may bear leaves unlike those of adults, and juvenile foliage can differ from the mature leaves used in identification keys. Flower shape, stem structure, and branching may also change. This is especially important in trees, climbing plants, and species with long juvenile phases. A specimen's stage of development must be recorded before it is compared with mature material.

Seasonal variation

The same individual can change substantially during a growing season. Leaves emerge with different colours or textures; flowers open and close; fruits change shape and colour as they mature. A collection made entirely from flowering specimens may conceal characteristics visible only in young growth or ripe fruit. Comparisons are most useful when the plants have been observed at comparable stages.

Heritable variation

Some differences persist across generations. They may form a continuous range through a population or occur in recognisable groups. Heritable variation supplies material upon which evolutionary processes act and can contribute to the emergence of geographically or ecologically distinct populations. Yet an inherited difference does not automatically justify a new species. Its significance depends on the character, its distribution, its relationship to other differences, and the biological history of the plants.

10.3. Clines, Populations, and Geographical Differences

Variation is not always distributed randomly. Across a large geographical area, leaf size may increase with rainfall, flowering time may shift with latitude, or flower colour may change gradually. A gradual geographical pattern of variation is called a cline.

Imagine a shrub ranging from dry southern foothills to wet northern forests. Southern populations have narrow leaves and compact growth; northern populations have broader leaves and taller stems. Between the extremes, intermediate forms are common. Studying only the two ends of this range might suggest two species. Examining the intervening populations can reveal a continuous series.

A cline does not prove that all populations belong to one species. Distinct species can occur near one another, and populations within a species can become strongly differentiated. Conversely, geographical isolation may allow populations to accumulate differences in morphology, ecology, and reproductive biology over long periods. Two populations may look nearly identical yet belong to distinct evolutionary lineages; markedly different populations may still exchange genes.

When examining geographical variation, establish whether intermediate populations exist, whether differences remain consistent across many individuals, whether environmental conditions explain part of the pattern, whether reproductive or ecological characteristics differ, and whether independent evidence supports the proposed boundaries. Distribution is important evidence, but separation on a map is not automatic proof of species status.

10.4. When Does a Variety Become a Species?

Botanical classification includes ranks below species, among them subspecies, variety, and form. These ranks can describe some kinds of variation without treating every difference as evidence of a separate species. Nomenclatural rules govern how names are formed, but they do not provide a universal biological formula for deciding when a population deserves one rank rather than another.

A subspecies commonly recognises a distinguishable population or group of populations within a species, often associated with geographical or ecological differentiation. A variety may recognise a particular set of characteristics within a species. A form is generally used for a comparatively minor distinction, sometimes involving colour or another structural feature. These are broad conventions, not mechanical rules; their use varies among taxonomic groups and authors.

Consider a plant widespread in the northern valleys of the Known Realms. Most individuals have white flowers, but a small valley population consistently produces deep violet flowers. The plants otherwise resemble the widespread form and appear able to reproduce with it. The colour could be ordinary variation, an environmental response, a stable variety, or one visible feature of a more distinct lineage. Colour alone cannot settle the matter.

The botanist should investigate distribution, other morphological characters, stability across generations, and relationships with neighbouring populations. Further reproductive or genetic study may be warranted. A taxonomic rank should describe a defensible interpretation of the evidence, not reward a difference merely because it is interesting.

The Library retains historical classifications in which minor colour variations were given formal names. Some remain useful in horticulture; others were abandoned because their distinctions could not be maintained consistently. Such records are not worthless: they show what earlier botanists considered important and help explain why a classification changed. Revision should improve earlier work, not ridicule those who produced it.

10.5. Hybrids: When Two Lineages Meet

A hybrid arises when reproduction brings together genetic material from distinct parental lineages. In plants, hybridisation can occur between species and, in some circumstances, between more distantly related taxa. It is a natural biological process, not a failure of classification.

Some crosses produce weak or sterile offspring; others produce viable plants capable of reproducing. In certain cases, hybridisation contributes to new evolutionary lineages. A hybrid may combine features of both parents, such as the leaf shape of one and the flower colour of another. It need not be intermediate in appearance: inherited characteristics can combine in unexpected ways, and a hybrid may resemble one parent closely or differ from both.

Evidence for suspected hybrid origin may include a combination of diagnostic features from plausible parents, the presence of those parents in connected habitats, compatible flowering periods, unusual fertility or reproductive development, and genetic or cytological evidence consistent with mixed ancestry. Not every hybrid shows every feature, and no single feature is conclusive. Intermediate morphology can also result from ordinary variation; sterility can have other causes.

Field botanists should record plausible parental species separately whenever possible. Collecting only the unusual intermediate plant may preserve the mystery without providing enough evidence to investigate it. A useful record includes the surrounding vegetation, distance between potential parents, flowering periods, and the range of variation in the suspected hybrid population.

The question is not simply, What does this plant resemble? It is also, What processes could have produced it?

10.6. Naming and Recording Hybrids

Botanical nomenclature uses the multiplication sign, ×, in certain hybrid names. Its placement depends on whether the name refers to an interspecific or intergeneric hybrid and on the taxonomic treatment being followed.

A hypothetical field record might read: Vitraluma palustris × Vitraluma argentata — suspected hybrid. This notation records the proposed parental combination; it does not establish that the identification is correct or that the plant represents a formally recognised hybrid taxon.

Documenting a suspected hybrid is not the same as naming a taxon. A formal treatment requires a defensible description and appropriate nomenclatural procedures. Not every hybrid needs a separate formal name, and not every plant of hybrid origin is treated as a hybrid taxon.

The Department uses three internal working categories: suspected hybrid, when evidence is incomplete; confirmed hybrid origin, when the evidence supports mixed ancestry but the population's long-term status may remain uncertain; and established hybrid lineage, when a persistent, distinct population warrants consideration as an independently maintained lineage. These are record-keeping categories, not formal taxonomic ranks.

10.7. Beyond the First Generation

The first generation produced by a cross is only one stage in a hybrid's possible history. Some hybrids are sterile or have very low fertility. Others reproduce with one another or backcross with a parental species. Repeated backcrossing can introduce genetic material from one species into populations of another, a process known as introgression.

Introgression complicates identification because a plant may possess some characteristics associated with one species and others associated with another. Over generations, a population may no longer resemble a simple mixture of two parental forms. Different populations bearing the same name may also have different histories.

Another complication is polyploidy, the condition of possessing more than two complete sets of chromosomes. It occurs naturally in plants and can arise through chromosome duplication or through hybridisation followed by changes in chromosome number. Polyploidy has played an important role in plant evolution and can contribute to reproductive separation between populations.

A hybrid with duplicated chromosome sets may sometimes reproduce more successfully than the original hybrid and establish a lineage with a distinctive history. In other cases, polyploid populations remain connected to their relatives through complex reproductive patterns. Not every polyploid is a new species, and not every hybrid must remain subordinate to its parents. Classification depends on the biological evidence and the species concept being applied.

In some groups, evolutionary history is not a simple branching tree. Hybridisation creates connections between lineages, a pattern often described as reticulation. Classification must accommodate the history that occurred, not merely the diagram that would be easiest to draw.

10.8. Species Boundaries and Competing Interpretations

The word species can create a false sense of precision. In practice, the question is which differences matter, how consistently they are expressed, and what they reveal about the organisms being compared. No single criterion resolves every case.

Some taxonomists emphasise morphological differences; others give greater weight to evolutionary relationships, reproductive patterns, ecological specialisation, or genetic evidence. These approaches need not be mutually exclusive. Different methods can produce different classifications when a group has diversified recently, exchanges genetic material, or reproduces in unusual ways.

Imagine closely related plants along the western mountain chain. One taxonomist recognises three species because leaf structure, flower arrangement, and habitat differ consistently. A second recognises one species, arguing that intermediate populations connect the forms. A third recognises two species and treats one form as a geographical variety. Each interpretation must explain the evidence and account for contrary observations.

This does not make classification arbitrary. A useful revision identifies the specimens examined, explains which characters were considered diagnostic, describes how variation was assessed, and acknowledges limitations in the material. The aim is not to eliminate every disagreement, but to make disagreement productive.

10.9. The Dangers of Splitting and Lumping

Splitting occurs when a taxonomist recognises several taxa where another recognises fewer. Lumping combines taxa previously distinguished. Neither approach is automatically correct or incorrect.

Splitting may be justified when populations represent distinct lineages with consistent differences supported by several kinds of evidence. It may also create unnecessary names when ordinary variation is mistaken for separate species. Lumping may be justified when supposed species cannot be distinguished reliably, but it may conceal meaningful evolutionary diversity if distinct lineages are combined too readily.

An early botanist might describe four species of glass-marsh lanternflower from neighbouring valleys, each based on a few specimens and distinguished by pale leaves, narrow petals, short stems, or a particular fruit shape. A century later, a larger collection may reveal that these characteristics occur in varying combinations and do not form consistent groups. Combining the names could be reasonable, provided the original reference specimens and new material are compared carefully.

The opposite can also occur: a widespread species may prove to contain several distinct lineages with different ecological requirements and restricted distributions. A classification can become simpler or more complicated as knowledge improves. Neither outcome measures scientific progress by itself. The question is whether the revised classification explains the evidence more convincingly than the one it replaces.

10.10. When the Evidence Is Incomplete

Taxonomists rarely have every specimen, every population, and every stage of a plant's life cycle. A species may be known from one historical collection; its habitat may have disappeared; flowering material may be unavailable; or the region may be inaccessible. The investigator must establish what the available evidence actually supports.

A specimen may be identifiable to genus but not species. It may resemble a named species without providing enough evidence for a definite identification, or possess a combination of features absent from existing descriptions. Such limits should be recorded explicitly. Qualifiers such as cf. and aff. are sometimes used to communicate uncertainty or apparent relationship, but their meaning depends on context and they do not replace an explanation of what remains unresolved.

A provisional identification is not a failure. It states the current limits of an investigation. The more serious error is to conceal uncertainty beneath a confident name. The Library's catalogue retains provisional determinations and the history of later revisions so that researchers can see both the conclusion and the evidence behind it.

There are good reasons to postpone naming a new species. A name introduced without adequate evidence may duplicate an existing taxon or describe an unusual individual rather than a distinct species. Some discoveries require more specimens, better observations, or a return to the original locality. The absence of a new name does not diminish the value of careful discovery. Sometimes an expedition's most useful result is a question that can now be investigated properly.

10.11. Disputed Species in the Grand Library

The Department of Botany maintains a register of taxonomic disagreements. Rather than forcing every dispute into one accepted answer, the register records competing interpretations, evidence for each, and the work that might resolve the question. The following fictional cases illustrate recurring problems.

Case I — The Northern Glass-Marsh Lanternflower

Principal question: environmental variation or distinct species? Northern marsh plants have broader leaves and larger flowers than southern specimens. One interpretation treats them as a variable species; another recognises a northern taxon. Existing herbarium material shows a geographical pattern but does not establish whether the differences persist under comparable growing conditions. Further fieldwork and comparative cultivation are recommended.

Case II — The Ashen Bellflower

Principal question: hybrid origin or natural variation? A population combines traits found in two neighbouring species and occurs where their ranges overlap. Some individuals produce poorly developed fruits. Hybrid origin is plausible, but the material is insufficient to exclude ordinary variation or a previously overlooked lineage. The register retains a provisional determination.

Case III — The Three Names of the Red Thorn

Principal question: three species or one complex species? Three names were published for thorn-bearing shrubs from separate mountain valleys, based on spine length, leaf division, and fruit colour. Later collections show overlapping characters, but the populations have not been sampled evenly. The original reference specimens must be compared with new material before the names can be reassessed confidently.

None of these cases can be resolved simply by selecting the most familiar name. Each requires competing explanations to be identified, distinguishing evidence to be sought, and the limits of the available material to be stated. Linking names, specimens, and arguments also allows scholars to follow the history of a disagreement without pretending it has already been settled.

Classification can affect conservation and medicinal research. If a rare population is treated as an ordinary form of a widespread species, its restricted distribution may receive insufficient attention. Conversely, recognising too many narrowly defined species without adequate evidence can distort assessments of diversity. The consequences of classification extend beyond the herbarium cabinet.

10.12. A Practical Method for Investigating a Disputed Plant

  1. Confirm the observation. Re-examine the specimen, repeat measurements where possible, and check that the structures have been compared correctly.
  2. Establish the range of variation. Examine multiple individuals and record whether the feature is consistent, rare, continuous, or associated with a particular stage of development.
  3. Review the identification history. Compare descriptions, illustrations, herbarium specimens, and earlier determinations. Establish how the existing name has been applied.
  4. Investigate environment and distribution. Record habitat, range, associated vegetation, and growing conditions. Consider environmental variation and geographical isolation.
  5. Compare independent characters. Examine vegetative and reproductive structures and other relevant characteristics rather than relying on one convenient feature.
  6. Consider alternatives. Ask whether the plant represents ordinary variation, a geographical population, a hybrid, a distinct lineage, or an unresolved identity.
  7. Seek further evidence. Depending on the question, this may require fieldwork, cultivation, reproductive observations, chromosome analysis, or genetic research.
  8. Record the conclusion and its limits. State what the evidence supports, which alternatives remain possible, and what further work would be useful. Preserve provisional status in the catalogue.

This procedure is cautious by design. It does not require a taxonomist to delay every decision until every possible question has been answered. It requires the decision to remain proportionate to the evidence. A working classification must be useful, but usefulness is not the same as certainty.

10.13. The Taxonomist's Responsibility

There is satisfaction in recognising a plant that has puzzled other observers. There is also a temptation to claim more than the evidence supports, especially when a new species would bring recognition to its discoverer. The pressure can work in the opposite direction: a taxonomist may hesitate to propose a new species because it would challenge a familiar classification or an established authority.

Neither ambition nor reluctance should determine the result. The taxonomist's responsibility is to describe the evidence accurately, evaluate the available explanations, and make the reasoning open to examination. If later work shows that the boundaries were drawn incorrectly, the classification should be revised rather than defended merely because it was once accepted.

This requires a distinction between confidence and certainty. Confidence is justified when several independent lines of evidence support an interpretation. Absolute certainty is rarely available in the study of complex biological systems. A conclusion may be well supported and still remain open to revision; that does not make it weak.

The Grand Library preserves disputed classifications because the history of an argument is itself valuable evidence. Future researchers may discover that a minority interpretation was correct, or that both sides overlooked a third explanation. Disagreement should not be preserved for its own sake, but because the evidence, reasoning, and history of a decision may help someone understand the problem more fully.

The first lesson of taxonomy is that plants can be described. The second is that descriptions do not always divide them into convenient groups. Populations may vary continuously across a landscape; species may exchange genetic material; a hybrid may establish a persistent lineage while another disappears after one generation. These are not defects in the natural world. They are features of its history.

I have changed my mind about the identity of plants I once considered unmistakable. I have also retained identifications that others questioned, not because I was unwilling to revise them, but because the available evidence continued to support them. In both cases, the work depended on examining the material again. There is no shame in correcting a classification, and no virtue in preserving an error for the sake of consistency.

A species name is a useful conclusion, but it is not a promise that nature will remain within the boundaries we have drawn. When a plant refuses to fit, the first response should not be to force it into place. The first response should be to look more closely.

10. Classification Across Ecosystems

On the relationship between plants and their environments, the limits of geographical classification, and the danger of mistaking adaptation for ancestry.

11.1. The Landscape Is Not a Taxonomic System

A traveller returning from the northern forests might describe their plants as tall, dark-leaved, and tolerant of long winters. A merchant crossing the southern deserts might speak of thorned shrubs, fleshy stems, and flowers that appear only after rain. A physician working along the eastern marshes might distinguish plants by the depth of water in which they grow.

These descriptions are useful. They tell us something about where plants live and how they may be encountered. They do not, by themselves, tell us how the plants are related.

Ecology investigates relationships between organisms and their environments, including the effects of climate, soil, water, other organisms, and disturbance. Taxonomy concerns the recognition, description, naming, and classification of organisms. The two disciplines frequently inform one another, but their conclusions should not be confused.

Consider three unrelated plants found in dry regions of the Known Realms. All possess small leaves, protective surface structures, and slow growth during periods of drought. Their similarities might suggest a close relationship to an inexperienced observer. Yet one is a shrub descended from woodland plants, another belongs to a group of succulents, and the third is a perennial herb whose above-ground growth disappears during the driest months.

This resemblance, when it results from similar environmental pressures acting on different lineages, is known as convergent evolution. Organisms facing similar challenges may evolve comparable solutions without inheriting those solutions from a recent common ancestor. In plants, these similarities may involve water storage, leaf reduction, climbing structures, growth form, or the timing of reproduction.

A plant's habitat may help us identify it, and its adaptations may explain why it thrives in a particular place. Neither fact automatically establishes its taxonomic identity. The landscape shapes the problems plants must solve; it does not dictate a single taxonomic answer.

11.2. Climate, Soil, and the Conditions of Growth

Plants do not experience an ecosystem as a name on a map. They experience temperature, light, water availability, soil chemistry, wind, competition, herbivory, and many other conditions that change across space and time.

Two plants growing only a short distance apart may experience very different environments. One may root in deep, moist soil beneath a canopy; another may grow in a shallow crack exposed to direct sunlight. Their appearance may reflect those differences as much as their inherited characteristics.

Habitat descriptions should therefore be more precise than broad labels such as forest plant, marsh plant, or mountain species. A taxonomist examining a plant from the eastern wetlands should record whether it grows in permanently flooded ground, seasonally inundated margins, saturated peat, or relatively dry banks.

Soil is equally important. Acidity, salinity, nutrient availability, texture, drainage, and the presence of particular minerals can influence plant growth. Some species tolerate a broad range of conditions; others are restricted to specialised habitats. A plant found on mineral-rich rock may be absent from the surrounding soil because the local substrate does not meet its requirements.

The Grand Library's ecological records follow a simple rule: describe the immediate growing conditions before assigning a plant to a broader habitat category. A note stating that a shrub grows on a north-facing slope, in shallow limestone soil beneath an open canopy, is more useful than one stating only that it grows in a mountain forest.

11.3. Forests: Competition, Light, and Layered Growth

Forests are not uniform environments. Light, moisture, temperature, and wind vary between the canopy, understory, forest floor, and exposed edges. These differences can produce a wide range of plant forms within a relatively small area.

Tall trees compete for light and occupy the upper layers. Shrubs and young trees grow beneath them, while herbs, ferns, mosses, and seedlings persist closer to the ground. Climbers use other plants for support, and epiphytes grow upon trunks or branches without necessarily drawing nutrients directly from their hosts.

Each growth form presents different challenges for observation. A canopy tree may be difficult to collect in flower. A climber may be mistaken for a separate plant if its connection to the supporting tree is not traced carefully. An epiphyte may appear to grow from a branch when its roots are attached to bark or trapped in accumulated organic matter.

Plants beneath a dense canopy may develop larger or thinner leaves than individuals exposed to stronger light. Some produce longer stems or arrange their leaves to capture limited light. These differences can be substantial without indicating separate species. A taxonomist should determine whether the characteristics remain consistent across different light conditions.

Climbing plants offer a clear example of convergence. Different lineages may independently evolve twining stems, tendrils, hooks, or other structures that help them reach sunlight without supporting the full weight of an upright trunk. Similar function does not prove identical origin.

A forest is also a community. Plants interact through competition, shading, pollination, seed dispersal, and relationships with fungi and animals. Such relationships can help explain distribution, but they should not be mistaken for taxonomic evidence on their own.

11.4. Marshes and Wetlands: A Habitat Defined by Water

Water shapes wetland vegetation in ways that are not always obvious from the surface. A marsh may contain permanently submerged plants, floating vegetation, rooted plants with leaves above the water, and species that occupy wet margins only during particular seasons.

Even within one marsh, water depth, current, oxygen availability in the soil, and duration of flooding can vary considerably. Plants adapted to waterlogged conditions may possess specialised tissues that help transport oxygen through submerged or poorly aerated parts. Others survive seasonal flooding by maintaining underground organs, producing tolerant seeds, or completing their life cycles when conditions are suitable.

Some aquatic and amphibious plants display striking differences between submerged and emerged growth. A taxonomist who collects only one form may mistake these differences for distinctions between species. Wetland expeditions should record water depth, seasonal conditions, substrate, and whether the collected material was submerged, floating, or growing above the waterline.

Wetlands also change with rainfall, flooding, sediment accumulation, and human intervention. A species occupying a shallow margin in one year may find that margin submerged the next. Historical specimens can reveal populations that no longer exist at a recorded location, but absence from a recent survey does not always prove disappearance: the plant may be dormant, seasonally absent above ground, difficult to detect, or overlooked.

In the Known Realms, the eastern Glass Marsh provides a useful example. Vitraluma palustris is recorded from several shallow marshes, where it grows among reeds and sedges. Some populations occupy exposed margins that dry during late summer; others occur in more persistent wet ground. Plants from drier margins tend to be shorter and have narrower leaves, while individuals from deeper water may develop longer stems and broader foliage.

At present, the evidence does not justify a final division of these populations. The department maintains separate ecological records, links them to herbarium specimens, and treats the morphological differences as a subject for further investigation. Habitat data may help explain variation, but they do not establish that water depth is its sole cause.

11.5. Mountains: Elevation, Isolation, and Short Growing Seasons

Mountain environments change rapidly with elevation. Temperature generally decreases with altitude, while exposure to wind, snow, sunlight, and changes in moisture create additional differences between slopes and ridges. Forests may give way to scrub, alpine meadows, exposed rock, and sparse vegetation near the highest elevations.

Some mountain plants grow close to the ground, forming compact cushions or mats. Others develop deep roots or flexible stems that withstand wind and shifting substrate. Many complete flowering and seed production during a relatively short favourable season. These characteristics can be useful for identification, but a high-altitude plant may be a distinct lineage or a high-altitude population of a more widespread species.

Mountain ranges can separate populations even when the distances between them appear small on a map. Valleys, unsuitable soils, and differences in climate may restrict the movement of seeds or pollen. Over time, isolated populations may accumulate distinctive characteristics. Yet isolation is not proof of taxonomic distinction: some populations remain similar despite long separation, while others differ markedly over short distances.

Within a single slope, plants may occupy moist crevices, dry scree, snowmelt channels, exposed ridges, or sheltered hollows. Recording only the mountain name loses much of this information. Precise habitat notes may be crucial for understanding distribution and guiding conservation, provided sensitive locality data are handled responsibly.

11.6. Deserts and Drylands: Survival Without a Single Solution

Drylands impose strong constraints on water availability, but plants respond to drought in many different ways. Some store water in fleshy leaves or stems. Others reduce exposed leaf area, develop protective surfaces, or invest in extensive root systems. Some survive unfavourable periods as seeds or underground organs and grow rapidly when rain arrives.

No single feature defines all desert plants. Succulent stems have evolved in multiple lineages. Reduced leaves may lower water loss, yet they may also occur for reasons unrelated to drought. Deep roots can provide access to water, while shallow roots may be advantageous where brief rainfall events wet only the upper soil.

Not every desert plant survives drought by tolerating it directly. Some annual species germinate after rainfall, grow quickly, flower, and produce seeds before the ground dries again. Their above-ground lives may be brief, while their seeds remain dormant through long periods of unfavourable conditions. A field survey conducted during a dry month may therefore record no living individuals even though the species remains present in the soil seed bank.

The Known Realms contain several dryland plants with thick stems, reduced leaves, and pale outer surfaces. Early catalogues grouped them under the broad heading “stone succulents”. The category was useful to travellers seeking water-storing plants, but taxonomically unhelpful: it included plants from several unrelated lineages, each with different floral structures and reproductive organisation. Modern catalogues retain the historical heading as a search term, not as a natural taxonomic group.

11.7. Coastal Habitats: Salt, Wind, and Moving Ground

Coastal habitats are shaped by salt exposure, wind, shifting sand, tidal movement, and differences in freshwater availability. A plant growing on a sheltered coastal slope may experience conditions quite unlike those of a species occupying a tidal flat only a short distance away.

Salt-tolerant plants, often called halophytes, possess adaptations that allow them to survive where salinity would damage many other species. Depending on the plant, these may involve regulating salt uptake, storing ions in particular tissues, excreting salt, or tolerating higher internal salt concentrations. These characteristics are not universal and should not be treated as diagnostic without further evidence.

Dunes present different challenges. The substrate may shift, water drains rapidly, and wind can bury or expose stems and roots. Plants that stabilise dunes may develop extensive root systems or spreading underground stems. By trapping sand and reducing movement, vegetation can help create conditions in which other plants establish.

The distribution of coastal plants may also follow the frequency and duration of tidal flooding. Some species tolerate regular immersion, while others occupy ground reached only during exceptional tides or storms. The Grand Library's coastal records include the approximate elevation of a collection site relative to the local tidal range when measurements are available. At minimum, field notes should state whether a plant was regularly submerged, reached by spray, or growing beyond normal tidal influence.

11.8. Rivers, Caves, and Other Specialised Environments

Not every plant habitat fits neatly into the familiar categories of forest, marsh, mountain, or desert. Riverbanks are shaped by flooding, erosion, and sediment deposition. Plants may be adapted to periodic disturbance, growing quickly on newly exposed ground or surviving burial beneath deposited material. Rivers may also act as corridors for dispersal, allowing species to spread along their banks.

Caves and deep rock shelters present different challenges. Light may be scarce or absent, moisture can be persistent, and organisms found there may include plants near entrances as well as mosses, algae, and other photosynthetic organisms occupying illuminated surfaces. A claim that a plant grows in complete darkness requires careful scrutiny: photosynthetic plants generally require light, and apparent growth in darkness may reflect stored resources, an unnoticed light source, or an incorrect identification.

The Grand Library also maintains records from geothermal slopes, mineral springs, and regions where unusual environmental conditions occur naturally. Such places can support specialised plant communities, but unusual habitat alone does not establish that their inhabitants are magical or taxonomically unique. The first task is to describe the conditions accurately; the second is to determine whether the plant's characteristics differ consistently from those of related taxa.

11.9. Magical Environments and Unusual Ecological Claims

The Known Realms contain habitats in which magical influences have been documented, suspected, or disputed. Some regions possess persistent environmental effects; others are associated with isolated reports that have not been independently confirmed. The Department of Botany does not exclude magical influences from ecological investigation. It does, however, require researchers to distinguish direct observations from explanations proposed to account for them.

Suppose a plant grows near an ancient warding stone and produces flowers outside its usual season. Several possibilities deserve consideration. The plant may respond to local temperature or moisture conditions. The site may provide unusual light exposure or soil chemistry. The population may possess inherited differences in flowering time. The event may have been recorded incorrectly, or the plant may indeed respond to a magical influence associated with the site. The observation alone does not determine which explanation is correct.

A useful investigation would compare the plant with populations growing in similar habitats without the suspected influence. Researchers would record flowering dates over multiple seasons, examine morphology, and document environmental conditions. If a magical effect could be measured or manipulated safely, its relationship to the observed change could be investigated directly.

The purpose of this procedure is not to deny the possibility of magic. It is to prevent the word magic from becoming a substitute for investigation. Plants reported to glow, change colour in response to speech, grow unusually quickly, or appear only under particular celestial conditions should be documented in enough detail that another researcher can understand what was observed and, where possible, attempt to reproduce it.

The taxonomic question must also remain separate from the ecological one. A plant that glows at night may still belong to a familiar species. An unusual environmental response does not automatically establish a new genus or species. Conversely, a plant may represent a distinct lineage even if its most unusual ecological behaviour proves to be a mistaken report.

11.10. Ecological Niches and the Distribution of Species

A species' ecological niche is not simply the place where it lives. It encompasses the conditions and resources associated with its survival and reproduction, together with its interactions with other organisms.

Two species may occupy the same geographical region but use different resources or tolerate different environmental conditions. One may flower early in the year, another later. One may require exposed ground, while another establishes only beneath a canopy. Conversely, species with similar ecological requirements may occupy entirely different regions because geographical barriers, historical events, or limited dispersal prevent them from reaching the same places.

A map showing where a species has been collected describes recorded occurrences. It does not necessarily reveal every place where the species could survive, nor explain why it is absent from apparently suitable habitat. Absence can reflect inadequate survey, seasonal dormancy, limited dispersal, competition, disease, unsuitable microclimate, or local disappearance.

Herbarium data can reveal patterns across time and space, especially when records include reliable locality and habitat information. Nevertheless, historical records are uneven. Some regions have been collected extensively, while others remain poorly represented. Frequently visited roads, settlements, and botanical gardens may have many records simply because collectors could reach them easily.

The map of botanical knowledge is not always the same as the map of botanical diversity.

11.11. The Problem of Ecotypes

An ecotype is a population or group of populations within a species associated with particular environmental conditions and displaying characteristics related to those conditions. The term is most useful when the differences have a heritable component rather than arising entirely from immediate environmental effects.

A widespread grass, for example, may occur in both dry uplands and damp lowlands. Upland populations may flower earlier and possess narrower leaves, while lowland populations flower later and grow taller. If these differences persist when plants are grown under comparable conditions, they may indicate inherited differentiation associated with their environments. If the differences disappear, the original appearance may have been largely a response to local growing conditions.

The distinction is not always easy to establish. A plant's appearance in its natural habitat reflects both inherited characteristics and the conditions in which it developed. Researchers may need repeated observations, cultivation experiments, or other evidence to determine how much each factor contributes.

Even when an ecotype is well supported, it does not automatically require a formal taxonomic name. Ecological differentiation can occur within a species without creating a distinct subspecies or variety. Conversely, taxonomists should not dismiss ecological specialisation simply because morphological differences are subtle.

The Grand Library's records distinguish ecological observations from taxonomic decisions. A population may be described as associated with drought or adapted to saline ground without being assigned a new taxonomic rank.

11.12. Building an Ecological Record

A botanical collection becomes far more useful when its specimens are linked to reliable ecological records. The following example illustrates how the Department of Botany documents a plant whose appearance varies across habitats.

Grand Library · Ecological Field Register

Preliminary Interpretation

The observed differences are compatible with habitat-associated variation, but the available records do not establish their cause. Further observations and comparative studies are required before a taxonomic distinction can be considered.

The next stage might involve revisiting both sites at comparable stages of development, measuring more individuals, examining reproductive structures, or cultivating material under similar conditions. The ecological record remains valuable even if the populations are ultimately classified as one species: it documents the range of conditions in which the plant occurs and helps future researchers interpret its variation.

11.13. Ecological Adaptation Is Not a Shortcut to Classification

The relationship between ecology and taxonomy can be summarised through three questions.

What is the plant? This is the taxonomic question. It requires identification and comparison with known taxa, using relevant morphological, reproductive, evolutionary, and other evidence.

Where does the plant grow? This is the distributional question. It requires reliable locality records and an understanding of the geographical range represented by those records.

Why does the plant grow there? This is the ecological question. It may involve climate, soil, competition, dispersal, reproductive relationships, historical events, and many other factors.

The questions inform one another, but none automatically answers the others. A plant may be identified confidently while its ecological requirements remain poorly understood. Its distribution may be well documented even though the reasons for its range are uncertain. Its adaptation to a specialised habitat may be obvious while its precise relationship to other taxa remains disputed.

If two plants grow in similar habitats, that similarity may reflect shared ancestry, convergent evolution, or a combination of historical and environmental influences. If a population occupies an unusual habitat, the fact may be important without necessarily justifying a new taxonomic name. Classification should reflect the organism's relationships, not merely the environment in which it happens to live.

11.14. A Working Method for Ecological Taxonomy

When habitat information may influence a taxonomic decision, the Department of Botany recommends the following approach.

  1. Describe the immediate environment. Record substrate, moisture, light, exposure, elevation where relevant, and the plant's position within the habitat.
  2. Observe multiple individuals. Determine whether the apparent characteristics are consistent or represent a limited part of the population's variation.
  3. Compare populations under similar conditions. Avoid attributing differences to geography or ancestry when specimens were collected at different developmental stages or under markedly different conditions.
  4. Distinguish environmental response from inherited difference. Use repeated observations or suitable comparative methods where the distinction matters.
  5. Consider convergent evolution. Similar habitats may produce similar growth forms in unrelated groups.
  6. Examine more than ecological traits. Compare diagnostic morphology and other relevant evidence before drawing taxonomic conclusions.
  7. Record uncertainty. If the cause of a difference is unknown, state that clearly rather than choosing an explanation prematurely.
  8. Link the ecological record to physical evidence. Ensure specimen identifiers connect field notes, images, and locality records.

This method is not a substitute for specialised ecological research. It is a framework for ensuring that ecological observations contribute to taxonomy without being mistaken for a complete taxonomic argument. The investigator should be willing to revise the interpretation when new evidence becomes available.

11.15. The Taxonomist's Responsibility

The Known Realms cannot be divided into botanical territories in which every plant belongs to a predictable category. Forests contain plants with different histories. Deserts produce similar solutions in unrelated lineages. Mountains isolate populations, rivers connect habitats, and wetlands change their boundaries from one season to the next.

A landscape provides the conditions under which plants live. It also shapes the evidence available to the botanist. Some species are easy to observe throughout the year; others appear only briefly, grow beyond reach, or survive underground during unfavourable seasons. The records we collect are therefore influenced by both the plants themselves and the circumstances of our investigations.

This is why I distrust descriptions that begin and end with habitat. To call a plant a marsh species may be accurate, but it does not tell us whether its nearest relatives also grow in marshes. To describe a shrub as drought-adapted may explain some of its features, but it does not establish its place in a taxonomic hierarchy. To find a population on a remote mountain does not prove that it is a distinct species.

These observations are beginnings, not conclusions. The botanist must move between scales: from the individual leaf to the whole plant, from the individual plant to the population, and from the population to the landscape. Each scale reveals information that the others cannot provide alone.

The Grand Library's ecological collections are valuable because they preserve these connections. A pressed plant without a habitat record may still assist identification. A habitat description without a reliable identification may still document a place. Together, carefully linked, they can help explain both the distribution of a species and the variation found within it.

There is no single landscape that teaches us how to classify all plants. There is only the continuing work of observing what grows, recording the conditions in which it grows, and comparing that evidence with what we already know.

The forest, the marsh, and the desert do not arrange themselves according to our catalogues. It is our responsibility to make the catalogues accurate enough to serve them.

11. Taxonomy of Magical and Anomalous Plants

“The extraordinary does not excuse us from being precise.”

— Professor Elowen Thorne, field notebook, eastern marshes.

12.1. The Problem of the Unusual Plant

Every botanical collection eventually acquires a specimen that seems to resist explanation. A flower may close when approached by a particular animal; a shrub may produce leaves of two distinct colours under apparently identical conditions; a vine may withdraw its tendrils from one surface and cling firmly to another. In the Known Realms, some plants exhibit phenomena that cannot readily be explained through ordinary botanical observation alone.

Such specimens are frequently described as magical, enchanted, anomalous or unnatural. These terms are convenient, but they are not interchangeable, and none should be mistaken for a taxonomic determination. A plant is not a new species simply because it behaves unusually. Nor does unusual behaviour necessarily indicate magic. The first task is to establish what the plant is; the second is to determine precisely what makes it unusual.

Consider a plant discovered beside an old boundary stone. Its flowers emit faint blue light after sunset, and local accounts attribute this to the stone. Three questions must be separated: is the plant already known; is the luminous behaviour genuine and repeatable; and, if confirmed, what causes it? Only the first belongs directly to taxonomic identification. The others concern verification and further investigation. I have seen researchers answer the third question before investigating the first two. I have done so myself.

12.2. What Do We Mean by “Magical”?

The Department of Botany uses magical plant as a practical descriptive category for plants whose observed properties appear to involve a demonstrable magical influence. It is not a formal taxonomic rank. A known marsh plant might glow only when exposed to a particular magical field without ceasing to belong to its established species. Conversely, a plant resembling a familiar species may possess a stable combination of structural, reproductive and inherited characteristics that distinguishes it from every known relative.

These are investigative categories used by the Grand Library, not formal ranks such as species, genus or variety. A known species may constitute an anomalous population in one region, while a separate investigation establishes that its behaviour is associated with a measurable magical influence. The catalogue must record each conclusion independently.

12.3. Establishing the Plant’s Identity

Before investigating a reported anomaly, examine the plant as any other botanical specimen. Record its growth form, stem structure, leaf arrangement, venation, reproductive organs, fruit, seeds, roots and other diagnostic features. Note its developmental stage and the conditions under which it was observed. Compare it with existing descriptions, authenticated herbarium material and closely related taxa.

Do not begin by searching for a magical explanation. Begin by asking whether the specimen has been correctly identified. Travellers, healers, spellwrights and local residents may provide valuable evidence, but a name attached to a specimen may reflect local tradition rather than botanical identity. One common name can refer to several unrelated plants, while one species may have many names across different communities. The Grand Library retains local names but does not treat them as conclusive identifications.

The minimum evidence record

  • Specimen identity: collection number, provisional identification and comparison material.
  • Morphology: physical features, including unusual structures.
  • Location: locality where safe and appropriate, habitat and associated vegetation.
  • Conditions: season, time, weather, soil moisture, light and relevant environmental measurements.
  • Phenomenon: exactly what was observed, without assuming its cause.
  • Repetition: whether it occurred once or on several occasions.
  • Comparison: whether similar plants without the characteristic were examined.
  • Evidence: notes, drawings, photographs, measurements, recordings or suitable specimens.
  • Uncertainty: unanswered questions, conflicting observations and alternative explanations.

“The flower glowed with ancient magic” is not an adequate botanical record. A useful entry would read: “At 21:14, the open corollas of six mature plants emitted faint blue-green light visible in low ambient illumination. The effect persisted for approximately eleven minutes. No comparable light was observed in four nearby individuals of the same provisional identification. The cause remains undetermined.” The second statement is less dramatic, and considerably more useful.

12.4. Distinguishing Structure, Behaviour and Cause

Structural anomalies

These concern physical form: unusual leaf arrangement, additional floral organs, unexpected root patterns or fruits containing structures absent from related specimens. Determine whether the feature is consistent and whether it could result from injury, disease, developmental disturbance or environmental stress. A single malformed flower is rarely sufficient evidence for a new species.

Physiological anomalies

These concern plant function, such as unusually rapid tissue regeneration, unexpected tolerance of extreme temperatures or production of a substance under conditions in which related plants do not produce it. Document the conditions and avoid interpreting one individual as representative of an entire species.

Behavioural anomalies

Plants may respond to light, touch, vibration, temperature, chemical signals or other organisms. Movement alone is not proof of magic. A tendril changing direction after contact should first be studied as a response to a stimulus. If its behaviour differs from that of related species, measure and document the difference.

Environmental anomalies

A plant may occur in an unexpected location, such as a warm-lowland species growing on a cold mountain ridge. Verify the locality, identification and conditions first: a microhabitat, unusual water source or misleading label may explain an apparently impossible distribution.

Magical anomalies

This category is reserved for cases in which evidence supports a connection between the plant and a magical influence. Document the suspected influence, conditions and repeatability; where appropriate and safe, compare exposed and unexposed plants. Even when an association is established, the mechanism may remain unknown. Evidence that a phenomenon occurs is not necessarily evidence of why it occurs.

12.6. Case Study I — The Blue-Veined Marsh Lanternflower

Register: GLB-A-052 · Provisional identification: Vitraluma palustris · Location: Eastern marshes, lower reed basin · Status: A — Known Taxon, Unusual Characteristic.

The marsh lanternflower is a useful example because its reputation considerably exceeds the available evidence. Local accounts describe it as a guide for travellers who have lost their way. Some claim it shines more brightly for those who approach with honest intentions; others say its flowers change colour in response to spoken promises. The reliability of these accounts varies considerably.

My investigation began with a modest observation: several plants beside a shallow channel possessed more pronounced blue-green veins than those in the Department’s existing specimens. At first I suspected leaf age. Later observations suggested a more complicated pattern, with coloration varying between individuals and apparently associated with growing conditions.

Nocturnal light required a separate investigation. During one visit, six mature plants displayed visible luminescence. On a subsequent visit under apparently similar conditions, only two did. I was tempted to attribute the difference to fluctuations in the marsh’s magical currents, but the measurements were insufficient.

The record therefore distinguishes three findings: the plants correspond morphologically to material identified as Vitraluma palustris; blue-green venation occurs in the observed population, varying between individuals; and nocturnal luminescence has been recorded, but its distribution, consistency and cause remain incompletely understood.

The evidence does not justify proposing a new species, nor does it establish that the flowers respond to intention or spoken promises. Legends remain valuable records of local knowledge and possible guides for future observation, but they are not substitutes for it.

I once stayed beside the eastern channel until dawn, convinced that the flowers would brighten when sunlight touched the water. They did not. I had mistaken the gradual fading of the night sky for a response from the plants. I recorded the failure, although I confess I was less pleased to do so than I ought to have been.

— Elowen’s field notebook

12.7. Case Study II — The Glass-Moon Orchid

Register: GLB-A-117 · Provisional identification: Orchidaceae, genus unresolved · Location: Upper limestone caverns · Status: C — Candidate New Taxon.

The Glass-Moon Orchid is known from a small number of specimens collected in caverns beneath the northern limestone range. Its translucent petals and reflective reproductive structures have led writers to call it a flower made from living crystal. That description is misleading: the available material is organic plant tissue, although it differs markedly in appearance from related orchids.

The petals are thin and nearly colourless, with visible veins. Its reproductive structures show a distinctive arrangement that may prove taxonomically significant. Yet most specimens were collected in low light, and the flowers change appearance after removal from the cavern. Early drawings show rigid, glasslike petals; later observations suggest that illumination angle and surface moisture may partly explain the effect.

The specimens are incomplete, no mature fruit has been documented in material available to the Department, and the relationship with the nearest known orchid relatives remains unresolved. “Glass-Moon Orchid” is therefore retained as a provisional common name; no formal scientific name is proposed here.

Further research should prioritise non-destructive observations of living plants, documentation of reproductive development and comparison with authenticated orchid material. Collection requires appropriate permission and particular care given the limited known population. It would be premature to declare a new species merely because it is beautiful, rare or difficult to study. Rarity makes evidence harder to obtain; it does not lower the standard of evidence required.

12.8. Case Study III — The Listening Reed

Register: GLB-A-203 · Provisional identification: Phragmites affinity, unconfirmed · Location: Western tidal flats · Status: D — Unresolved Botanical Phenomenon.

The Listening Reed takes its name from reports that its stems produce faint tones when someone speaks nearby. According to some accounts, the tones repeat fragments of the speaker’s words. Reports are inconsistent: some describe clear imitation, while others mention clicks, whistles or low vibrations that resemble syllables only after careful listening. No authenticated specimen has established the plant’s identity with confidence.

The effect may depend on wind passing through hollow stems, water moving through the reed bed, the acoustics of the terrain or a mechanism not yet documented. A magical response remains possible, but should not be the default explanation.

A responsible investigation would record the sounds and the conditions in which they occur, distinguishing what an observer heard from what they believed it resembled. Recordings should, where possible, be examined independently by people who have not been told which words the plant allegedly repeated. Once listeners expect speech, ordinary sounds can begin to resemble familiar words.

I have listened to recordings on four occasions. The first seemed unmistakably to contain my surname. On the second, I heard only wind; the third appeared to contain two syllables. On the fourth, after learning that the recording came from another day, I could no longer distinguish a consistent pattern. I have declined to catalogue this as a confirmed speaking species. The record remains open.

12.9. Designing an Investigation

  1. Secure the identification. Record morphology and compare existing descriptions and specimens. State uncertainty explicitly.
  2. Define the phenomenon. Replace vague descriptions with observable statements. “The plant reacts to fear” is an interpretation; “the leaves folded within thirty seconds of the observer approaching” is an observation.
  3. Establish the circumstances. Record time, environment, developmental stage and any known exposure to magical influences.
  4. Repeat the observation. Repetition helps distinguish a consistent characteristic from chance, misperception or a temporary condition.
  5. Compare. Observe other individuals and, where possible, related plants without the reported anomaly. Record differences in conditions.
  6. Preserve evidence. Keep notes, drawings, photographs, recordings and suitable voucher material. Non-destructive documentation may be preferable.
  7. Review alternatives. Consider environmental influences, disease, developmental variation, contamination, mislabelling and limitations of observation.
  8. Assign a provisional status. Separate what is established, what is plausible and what remains unknown; update the record as evidence changes.

These stages need not occur in a perfectly linear order. New evidence may require a return to an earlier step. That is not failure; it is how a sound investigation develops.

12.10. Safety, Ethics and the Limits of Collection

Anomalous plants may present risks not apparent from their appearance. Some may irritate skin, release volatile substances, react to heat or light, or respond unpredictably when damaged. Others may be rare, culturally significant or restricted to fragile habitats. Never assume an unknown plant is harmless because it resembles a familiar species.

Before collecting material, consider whether a specimen is necessary, whether a drawing or photograph would suffice, and whether the population can withstand removal. Obtain appropriate permissions and consult local knowledge where relevant. If a plant presents an uncertain hazard, avoid unnecessary contact and uncontrolled experiments; refer potentially dangerous material to qualified staff with suitable facilities.

The same principles apply to magical phenomena. Do not expose an organism to an unknown influence merely to produce a dramatic observation, or repeatedly damage it to test regeneration. Scientific curiosity does not remove the obligation to protect the subject of study.

Earlier interpretations, including incorrect ones, should remain traceable. A scientific catalogue must preserve the history of its conclusions, not merely those that survive.

12.12. When Does an Anomalous Plant Become a New Taxon?

No single unusual feature automatically makes a plant a new species. A taxonomic proposal must be supported by a coherent body of evidence showing that the material can be distinguished from recognised taxa. Depending on the question, evidence may include morphology, reproductive characteristics, genetic evidence, ecology, geographical distribution and comparison with existing collections.

A magical property may contribute if it is shown to be a consistent, biologically meaningful difference. It cannot replace the wider investigation. Imagine two populations of the same lanternflower. In the first, flowers glow only when exposed to a particular environmental influence, while morphology and reproductive characteristics otherwise match the known species. Without further evidence, luminescence alone may not justify a new species. In the second, plants display a consistent suite of differences in reproductive structures, development and biological relationship to their closest relatives. Comparative study may support taxonomic recognition.

In both cases, the unusual property deserves documentation. Only the second example presents a broader case for taxonomic separation. The exact decision depends on the organism and evidence; taxonomy is not a checklist in which one strange characteristic earns a new name.

Formal descriptions of new taxa must also follow applicable nomenclatural rules. In real-world botanical practice, the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants governs the naming of plants and related groups, while taxonomic research determines how organisms should be circumscribed and classified. The Department of Botany follows the same distinction: naming a taxon and deciding what constitutes that taxon are related but separate tasks.

12.13. The Researcher’s Responsibility

Plants that appear magical attract stories, and stories attract attention. Local accounts may preserve observations that have not entered the scientific record; the testimony of experienced healers, gardeners and field workers can direct researchers towards important questions. Difficulty arises when a compelling account becomes impossible to question.

A taxonomist must record an observation without accepting every interpretation attached to it, while remaining willing to recognise that a phenomenon may be genuine even when its explanation is unknown. These positions are not contradictory.

I have known scholars who dismissed every report of magical plant behaviour as superstition, and others who accepted every report as proof of an entirely new branch of botanical science. Both approaches are unhelpful. One closes the investigation before evidence can be examined; the other reaches a conclusion before evidence has earned it.

The more demanding position is to investigate carefully, describe precisely, compare honestly and leave the record open to revision. Sometimes a phenomenon will be confirmed while its cause remains unknown. Sometimes an extraordinary plant will prove to be familiar species observed under unfamiliar conditions. Sometimes discoveries will require existing classifications to change. The taxonomist must be prepared for all three.

12. How to Document an Unknown Species

“Discovery begins when we notice something unfamiliar. Scientific discovery begins when we make that unfamiliar thing available for examination by others.”

13.1. Before You Give It a Name

Finding a plant that does not resemble anything you recognise is an exciting moment. It is also the moment at which a botanist must become particularly careful.

A strange flower may belong to a familiar species growing under unusual conditions. A plant with unfamiliar leaves may be an immature form of a known organism. Two unrelated species may look almost identical, while individuals of the same species can differ considerably in size, colour or shape.

The first task is therefore not to name the plant. It is to document it.

In the Department of Botany, an unknown plant is assigned a temporary collection number before its identity has been established. This number connects the field notes, illustrations, photographs, preserved material and later observations. If the specimen is eventually identified, its records can be updated without losing the history of the investigation.

Provisional Collection Record

Collection code: GLB-BOT-0264

Institution: Grand Library · Botany · Collection 0264

Identification: Unidentified

Field name: Silver-Cleft Bellflower

Scientific identification: Undetermined

Status: Preliminary documentation complete; comparison required

The field name is only a convenient label. It does not establish that the plant belongs to the bellflower family, nor that its flowers are true bells. A provisional name should help researchers refer to the specimen without disguising the fact that its identity remains unknown.

Never allow a temporary name to become an identification simply because it has been used for a long time.

13.2. Step One: Observe the Whole Plant

Before examining individual leaves or flowers, take time to observe the entire organism.

Look at its overall growth form. Is it a herb, shrub, tree, climber or rosette-forming plant? Does it grow as a single individual, in clusters or through underground stems? Is it rooted in soil, attached to another plant or growing from rock?

Record the plant's approximate height and width, its general shape and the position of its leaves and reproductive structures. Then examine the details:

  • Roots: Are they fibrous, thickened, branching or specialised for attachment?
  • Stems: Are they woody, herbaceous, hollow, ridged, hairy or smooth?
  • Leaves: Are they simple or compound? How are they arranged along the stem? What is their shape, margin and venation?
  • Flowers: How many floral parts are present? Are the flowers solitary or grouped? Where do they emerge?
  • Fruits and seeds: What is their shape, size, texture and arrangement?
  • Surface features: Are there hairs, glands, waxes, spines, unusual markings or other structures?

Not every feature will be visible during the first visit. A plant may be sterile, dormant or too large to examine in its entirety. Record what is present and state what is missing. Do not fill gaps in the record with assumptions.

13.3. Step Two: Record the Habitat

A plant cannot be fully documented without recording where and how it grows.

Write down the locality as precisely as is appropriate for the investigation. Include the habitat type, elevation when available, soil or substrate, moisture conditions, exposure to light and nearby vegetation.

The difference between “found in a forest” and “growing on a damp limestone ledge beneath the eastern canopy of an old beech forest” may be important to a later researcher.

Record the date and time. Note whether the site is exposed to strong winds, seasonal flooding, persistent shade or unusual temperature changes. If the plant occurs close to a magical disturbance, enchanted structure or other potentially relevant influence, document the observation without assuming that the association proves causation.

Also record abundance. Is this the only individual observed? Are there dozens nearby? Does the plant form a continuous population, or is it restricted to a few isolated patches?

If the species appears rare, avoid unnecessary collection and consider whether revealing its exact location could put the population at risk.

Herbarium practice similarly emphasises accurate locality, date, collector details, habitat and observations that may be lost when the plant is dried.

13.4. Step Three: Make a Field Description

A field description should be precise enough that another botanist could recognise the plant from the written account, even without seeing your original specimen.

Avoid descriptions based only on impressions. “Beautiful leaves” is not diagnostic. “Leaves dark green above, pale beneath, elliptic, with finely serrated margins and a pointed apex” gives another researcher something they can examine.

Measurements are particularly useful. Use a ruler or callipers when appropriate, and record a range if several individuals have been measured.

Similarly, if the plant seems perennial but has not been observed across seasons, describe it as apparently perennial or leave its life cycle undetermined. Precision includes knowing when not to make a claim.

13.5. Step Four: Photograph and Illustrate

Photographs preserve the appearance of a plant at a particular moment. Illustrations can reveal its structure more clearly, especially when the artist deliberately separates overlapping parts or enlarges small diagnostic features.

For an unknown species, document the following views whenever possible:

  • Plate A — Whole plant and habitat: Show the complete growth form, its surroundings and its relationship to the ground or supporting surface. Include a scale reference in photographs whenever possible.
  • Plate B — Leaves and stems: Show leaf arrangement, venation, margins, stem structure and distinctive surface features. Include both sides of a leaf if they differ.
  • Plate C — Reproductive structures: Draw the flower from the front and side. If possible, include a longitudinal section showing the internal arrangement of its parts.
  • Plate D — Fruits and seeds: Record mature fruit, seed shape, surface texture and internal structures when available. These details can be essential for identification.

Every image should be linked to the same collection number. Record which individual was photographed or drawn, the date and any scale used.

For an illustrated manuscript, the plates can be attractive without sacrificing scientific usefulness. Keep the main figure visually clear, then add smaller details around it: a leaf underside, a flower section, a seed viewed from several angles and a simple scale bar.

A decorative illustration may communicate atmosphere. A diagnostic illustration must also communicate evidence.

13.6. Step Five: Preserve a Reference Specimen

When collection is lawful, safe and appropriate, a preserved specimen can allow other researchers to verify your observations.

A specimen retained for this purpose is commonly called a voucher specimen. Depending on the plant, it may consist of pressed and dried material, a preserved flower or fruit, or another suitable preparation.

Where possible, the specimen should include diagnostic structures. A leafy stem without flowers or fruits may be insufficient to identify a species reliably.

The preservation method depends on the organism. Large trees, succulent plants, delicate flowers and plants with unusual structures may require different approaches. Some details must be recorded before preservation because colour, texture, scent and form can change during drying.

A voucher should never be separated from its field data. The physical specimen and its record are parts of the same evidence.

Collection and Preservation Note

Do not collect protected or potentially hazardous plants without the necessary permissions and precautions. If collecting is inappropriate, use detailed photographs, drawings, measurements and non-destructive observations instead.

13.7. Step Six: Compare It With Known Species

Once the first observations have been recorded, begin the identification process.

Consult regional floras, botanical descriptions, identification keys, illustrations and properly identified herbarium specimens. Compare the unknown plant with its closest apparent relatives, not merely with plants that share a similar colour or general shape.

Some characters vary with age or environment. Others may appear distinctive until a larger range of specimens is examined. If a key leads to an impossible result, check the observations and the key's scope before concluding that the plant is new.

If you cannot identify the plant confidently, ask a specialist familiar with the relevant plant group or region to examine the evidence. An honest provisional identification is more useful than a confident but unsupported name.

13.8. Step Seven: Decide Whether It Might Be a New Species

An unknown plant is not automatically an undescribed species.

Before proposing a new taxon, investigate whether the plant has already been described under another name, whether older specimens exist in herbaria, and whether its differences can be explained by age, environmental conditions, hybridisation or ordinary variation.

If the evidence suggests a genuinely distinct species, the researcher must prepare a formal taxonomic treatment. This typically includes a diagnosis distinguishing the proposed species from similar taxa, a detailed description, comparison with close relatives, relevant specimen information and a nomenclatural type designated according to the applicable rules.

A holotype is the particular specimen designated as the name-bearing type when a new species is formally described under the botanical nomenclature code. It provides a permanent reference for applying that name.

Naming a plant is not the same as proving its distinctness. The name gives researchers a way to refer to the taxon; the evidence must justify the taxonomic decision.

13.10. Elowen's Final Advice

I once spent three weeks attempting to identify a plant whose leaves appeared to possess an unusual arrangement. I drew the stem repeatedly, measured the spaces between the leaves and compared the specimen with descriptions from five different regional floras.

Eventually, another botanist noticed that two stems had become entangled during collection. The apparent leaf arrangement belonged to two separate plants.

I had not discovered a new species. I had documented a specimen incorrectly.

The mistake was embarrassing, but it taught me something that has remained useful throughout my career: the quality of an investigation depends not only on what you notice, but on how carefully you establish that the thing you noticed is real.

When you find an unknown plant, resist the urge to decide what it means. Give it a number. Observe the whole organism. Describe its parts. Record its surroundings. Draw what you can see. Preserve suitable evidence, compare it with known material and write down your uncertainties.

If the plant is new to science, your records may help establish that fact. If it turns out to be a familiar species, your records will still have served a purpose.

The object of documentation is not to prove that every unfamiliar plant is extraordinary. It is to make sure that no important observation is lost simply because its meaning was not immediately understood.

13. Common Errors and Scientific Controversies

14.1. The Botanist Is Not an Exception to Nature

There is a particular comfort in finding a mistake in someone else's work. A misplaced specimen, an incorrectly identified flower, or a name that should never have been published can be easy to criticise when the person responsible is absent, retired, or long dead. The difficulty begins when the mistake is ours.

I have spent thirty-two years studying plants, and I have made errors throughout them. Some were ordinary errors of observation. Others were failures of patience, made because I wanted an answer before the evidence was ready. I record these incidents because a textbook that describes only successful investigations gives a false impression of scientific work.

The solution is not to demand that investigators never make mistakes. It is to build methods that make mistakes easier to recognise, correct, and learn from. A correction should not erase the history of an investigation; it should make that history intelligible.

14.2. Error One: The Plant I Had Already Decided to Find

During my third year in the Department, I examined herbarium specimens hoping to support a respected taxonomist's proposal for a new genus. I wanted the theory to be correct. When I found several specimens that appeared to match its illustrations, I entered them under the proposed genus and drafted an enthusiastic report.

My supervisor returned it with one question: “Have you examined the mature fruit?” I had not. Once I did, the apparent differences became unconvincing. The specimens belonged to known species, and I had selected them because they resembled what I expected to find.

The lesson: a hypothesis should guide an investigation, not determine what the investigator is permitted to see. I still ask myself whether I am looking at a specimen or looking for an argument.

14.3. Error Two: The Herbarium Cabinet That Lied

An old coastal collection contained a specimen whose leaves resembled those of a rare coastal species. Its label appeared to document an unusual inland population, and I entered that identification in the catalogue. A visiting specialist later noticed that the label had been attached to the wrong sheet. My specimen was a common woodland plant collected much farther inland.

The catalogue had faithfully repeated information that was already wrong. I had made the error more permanent by accepting it without testing it against the material. Since then, I compare labels with handwriting, mounting methods, accession records, and surviving correspondence when something appears inconsistent.

A label is part of a specimen's history. It is not a substitute for examining the specimen.

14.4. Error Three: The Plant That Was Two Plants

During an expedition along the Greywater River, I collected a flowering stem with broad leaves and assumed they belonged to one organism. For nearly a month I tried to make the specimen fit a single taxonomic description. Eventually, my field drawing revealed that the broad leaves emerged from a creeping stem, while the flowering stalk rose from nearby soil. I had pressed two entangled plants together.

The correction was simple once the mistake was recognised. The specimen was separated and both plants were identified independently. Since then, I trace stems to their points of origin whenever possible. If I cannot establish that two structures belong to the same plant, I record the uncertainty rather than joining them into a convenient whole.

A specimen must represent what the investigator claims it represents.

14.5. Error Four: The Flower That Appeared Only at Night

A gardener reported a shrub that flowered after sunset, supposedly only when a nearby magical boundary was active. I arrived with a theory already in mind. After observing the flowers open on several evenings, I regarded the pattern as encouraging evidence. Only later did I notice that the garden lamps were being extinguished at almost exactly the same time.

Further observations showed that the flowers opened at night even when the boundary was inactive. I could not establish the complete mechanism, but the claimed connection had not been demonstrated. I had given greater attention to observations that supported my preferred explanation and overlooked the conditions that challenged it.

The plant was still interesting. The failure of one explanation did not make the observation worthless. An explanation must survive the evidence that challenges it, not merely the observations that support it.

14.6. The Catalogue Error That Lasted Forty Years

The Grand Library once treated “Red Widow” as a single medicinal species across several districts. A later review found that the same common name referred to different plants in different communities. The catalogue had confused a name with the organism it was supposed to identify, and the error had spread into copied herbals and correspondence.

The team preserved the local usages while linking each one to specimens and descriptions that could be verified. Common names are valuable cultural and historical evidence, but one name may refer to several species, and one species may have many names. The context of each usage must be recorded.

14.7. When Reputation Becomes an Obstacle

Two senior botanists at the Grand Library once disagreed about pale-barked trees from the northern forests. Professor Merrow proposed three species restricted to different elevations; Professor Halden argued that they represented one variable species. Their disagreement became personal, and students began choosing sides according to which professor they admired.

A third researcher compared specimens from several elevations. Some differences corresponded closely to habitat; others remained consistent and required further study. The final revision was more complicated than either original proposal. Neither professor was entirely right, and neither was entirely wrong.

The aim of scientific review is not to eliminate disagreement. It is to make disagreement productive by stating diagnostic criteria, documenting contradictory evidence, and distinguishing observations from conclusions.

14.8. The Difference Between a Controversy and a Scandal

Researchers may disagree because they have examined different specimens, used different definitions, or weighed evidence differently. Such disagreement is not automatically misconduct. Other situations—knowingly omitting locality data, reporting a single observation as repeated, or refusing to correct a demonstrably false record—raise questions about the reliability of the evidence itself.

SituationAppropriate response
Legitimate disagreementPreserve competing interpretations, document the evidence, and identify what further work might resolve the issue.
Correctable errorAmend the record, explain the correction, and retain the history of the earlier identification.
Evidence of deliberate misrepresentationInvestigate carefully, establish what can be demonstrated, and take appropriate institutional action.

An institution should not accuse a researcher of misconduct merely because an interpretation proves incorrect. Equally, respect for an established scholar must not prevent correction of a demonstrably false record.

14.9. The Danger of Explaining Everything with Magic

Within the Known Realms, magic is a legitimate subject of investigation. Yet its existence creates a temptation: when a phenomenon is unfamiliar, it becomes easy to assign it to magic and stop asking questions. A spiral-shaped tree, a colour-changing marsh plant, or an unexpectedly germinating seed may have several possible explanations.

The reverse error is also possible. A scholar who refuses to investigate a reported magical influence may overlook a genuine phenomenon. The appropriate approach is neither to invoke magic whenever an explanation is missing nor to exclude it before the evidence has been examined.

I have adopted a rule for my own field notes: never write “magical” where a description of the observed effect would be more informative. Record the change, the conditions, and the evidence linking them. The word may belong in the interpretation, but it should not replace the observation.

14.10. Five Habits That Prevent Avoidable Errors

  1. Examine the whole plant. Do not identify a species from one striking leaf or flower when other diagnostic structures are available.
  2. Keep the original evidence. Preserve field notes, photographs, collection numbers, and correspondence so later researchers can understand the decision.
  3. Record uncertainty explicitly. Distinguish observation from reports, interpretations, and hypotheses.
  4. Seek independent comparison. A second investigator may notice a character the original collector overlooked.
  5. Correct errors visibly. Amend records while preserving the history of the correction.

These habits cannot eliminate every error. They can make errors easier to find before they spread.

14.11. The Correction Ledger

The Department of Botany maintains a correction ledger for significant changes to specimen identifications, catalogue entries, and published interpretations. Each entry records the original claim, the evidence prompting review, the revised assessment, and the person responsible for the correction. Disputed issues remain documented rather than being forced into artificial consensus.

Correction Ledger — CL-208

Original entry: Red Widow, identified as one medicinal species across three districts.

Problem: Historical specimens associated with the name did not consistently match the botanical description.

A correction should not be treated as proof that an institution has failed. An institution fails when it refuses to correct a demonstrable error or destroys the evidence needed to understand how it occurred.

14.12. A Note to the Young Taxonomist

When I began my career, I believed expertise meant recognising a plant quickly. I admired scholars who could glance at a specimen and name it without consulting a book. I no longer consider speed the most reliable sign of expertise.

Experience helps a botanist recognise patterns and narrow possible identifications. It does not remove the need for comparison. Sometimes an experienced researcher is better equipped to recognise how little can be concluded from the available evidence.

Do not defend a mistaken identification simply because you have entered it into the catalogue. Do not continue an experiment because you have invested too much time in your original theory. Do not treat criticism as an insult when it identifies a weakness that can be corrected.

And do not mistake embarrassment for evidence that you should stop. The ability to revise an opinion in response to evidence is part of the work. I still make mistakes. What I hope to avoid is repeating an error simply because I am unwilling to acknowledge it.

14.13. Concluding Remarks

The botanical record is not a monument to perfect knowledge. It is the accumulated work of people who have observed, collected, compared, argued, revised, and sometimes misunderstood the plants before them. Its strength lies not in the absence of mistakes, but in the possibility of correcting them.

A catalogue should tell us what is currently believed about a plant. A good catalogue should also make it possible to discover why that belief exists, how securely it is supported, and what might cause it to change.

I have learned to distrust the sentence “the matter is settled” when it is written without an explanation of the evidence. I have also learned not to be ashamed of the words “I was mistaken.” They are not the end of a scientific investigation. Sometimes they are the point at which the real work begins.

The careful botanist is not the one who never makes an error. It is the one who leaves enough evidence for the next botanist to find it.

14.1. Using This Volume

The preceding chapters have introduced the principles required to observe, describe, identify and classify the plants of the Known Realms. This final chapter gathers the terminology and reference conventions used throughout the volume into a practical working guide.

Botanical terminology can appear unnecessarily complicated to the inexperienced reader. A single structure may possess several descriptive characteristics, each expressed by a specialised term. Such language is not intended to make botanical study exclusive. Its purpose is to make descriptions precise enough that researchers working in different regions can understand one another.

When a description states that a leaf is opposite, lanceolate and serrulate, it conveys three distinct observations: the position of the leaf on the stem, its general shape and the form of its margin.

Learning these terms is therefore not separate from learning to observe. The vocabulary provides a way to record what the eye discovers.

This chapter is organised into five reference sections: a glossary of frequently used botanical and taxonomic terms; abbreviations and symbols used in botanical records; instructions for working with identification keys; standards for specimen records and catalogue entries; and a guide to the illustrated plates accompanying this volume.

The definitions are deliberately concise. They provide a working foundation rather than a complete dictionary of botanical science.

14.3. Abbreviations and Symbols

Abbreviations allow botanical descriptions to remain concise, but only when the reader understands their meaning. Their interpretation depends on context, and a catalogue should define any specialised shorthand it uses.

sp.

One species that has not been identified or specified. Example: Vitraluma sp.

spp.

Multiple species within a genus. Example: Vitraluma spp.

cf.

From Latin confer, meaning “compare with”; indicates an identification requiring comparison or expressing uncertainty.

aff.

From affinis, indicating affinity with a named taxon while suggesting that the specimen may differ from it.

subsp.

Subspecies, a taxonomic rank below species.

var.

Variety, a taxonomic rank below species.

f.

Form, a rank below variety in botanical taxonomy; the meaning must be distinguished from other uses of the abbreviation.

×

The multiplication sign, used in botanical nomenclature to indicate a hybrid or hybrid taxon in the appropriate context.

et al.

“And others”; used in scholarly references when listing multiple authors.

s.l.

Sensu lato, meaning “in the broad sense”.

s.s.

Sensu stricto, meaning “in the strict sense”.

In ordinary botanical usage, the abbreviations sp. and spp. are not italicised when appended to a genus name; the genus itself remains italicised. The multiplication sign also has specific nomenclatural conventions and should not be added casually to an informal plant name.

Writing Scientific Names

  • Genus names begin with a capital letter and are italicised: Quercus.
  • Specific epithets begin with a lowercase letter and are italicised: Quercus robur.
  • After the full name has been introduced, the genus may be abbreviated where the meaning remains unambiguous: Q. robur.
  • Common names, such as “English oak”, are not italicised.
  • A provisional identification should be labelled as provisional rather than presented as an established name.

The invented plant Vitraluma palustris may be used as an example throughout this volume, but its appearance in the manuscript does not constitute a formal scientific publication or establish a real-world taxonomic name.

14.4. How to Use a Dichotomous Identification Key

A dichotomous key guides the reader through a sequence of paired choices. At each step, the investigator selects the statement that best matches the specimen and follows the indicated route.

A key does not identify a plant by intuition. It identifies a possible match by applying a structured sequence of observations.

Illustrative Key: Unknown Herbaceous Plants

This simplified key is invented for teaching purposes and does not identify real species.

1a. Leaves divided into separate leaflets. Proceed to 2.

1b. Leaves undivided, with a single continuous blade. Proceed to 3.

2a. Leaflets arranged along a central axis. Provisional group: pinnately compound-leaved plants.

2b. Leaflets arise from a common point. Provisional group: palmately compound-leaved plants.

3a. Leaves opposite at the nodes. Provisional group: opposite-leaved plants.

3b. Leaves alternate at the nodes. Provisional group: alternate-leaved plants.

In this example, the key establishes broad morphological groupings rather than species-level identifications. A complete key would continue with additional characters and would need to define the plants it is intended to cover.

Rules for Using a Key Correctly

  1. Use an appropriate key. A regional flora may not include plants from distant climates or unusual habitats.
  2. Examine the specimen carefully. Determine whether the relevant feature is actually present and visible.
  3. Read both alternatives. Do not select the first statement that seems plausible without checking its counterpart.
  4. Follow the directions exactly. Each choice leads to a new step or a possible identification.
  5. Check the result independently. Compare the final identification with a full description, illustrations and authenticated specimens.
  6. Repeat the process if necessary. An incorrect choice early in the key can lead to a convincing but wrong result.

What if Neither Option Fits?

Stop. Do not force the specimen into the nearest alternative. Re-examine the relevant structure and consider whether it is damaged, immature, abnormal or incompletely observed. The key may not cover the plant, or the character may have been interpreted incorrectly.

Record where the key ceased to be useful. If a different key produces another result, document that too.

A key is an aid to identification, not an authority that overrides the specimen.

14.5. Standard Specimen Record Conventions

Every specimen in the Grand Library's herbarium should have a record that allows another researcher to establish what was collected, where it came from and how its identification was reached.

The minimum record should include:

  • A unique collection or accession number.
  • The collector's name and collection date.
  • The locality and habitat.
  • The observed growth form and other relevant field characteristics.
  • The scientific identification, including its degree of certainty.
  • The repository holding the specimen.
  • The history of subsequent identifications or taxonomic changes.

Additional data may include elevation, associated species, soil conditions, photographs, measurements, local names and information about the living plant that cannot be recovered from preserved material.

Example of a Specimen Citation

Vitraluma palustris — Eastern Marshes, lower reed basin, growing on saturated soil near a shallow channel; collected by E. Thorne, 17 Rainwane 814; field number GLB-BOT-0264; deposited in the Grand Library Herbarium, accession GLH-1842-0671.

Identification: Provisional. Nocturnal luminescence recorded in field notes.

This example uses the fictional calendar and institutions of the Known Realms. The accession number is illustrative.

Revising a Previous Identification

When a later researcher identifies a specimen differently, the new determination should be added to the record without destroying the original label or erasing the previous history.

An annotation should identify the revised determination, the person making it and the date. Where appropriate, it should also explain the evidence or publication supporting the change.

The purpose is not to preserve every old opinion as equally correct. It is to make the path from the original identification to the current one traceable.

14.6. Illustrated Plates: A Visual Reference System

The illustrated plates accompanying The Living Index should function as part of the scientific apparatus of the book, not merely as decoration.

A well-designed botanical plate allows the reader to compare structures quickly. It can emphasise diagnostic characters, show small features at a larger scale and present several views of the same organism without the visual distractions of its natural surroundings.

For an illustrated manuscript, the plates are organised into five groups.

Plate Group I — Morphology

Leaf shapes, margins, venation, root forms, stems, inflorescences, floral parts, fruits and seeds. Purpose: teach the reader to recognise and describe botanical structures.

Plate Group II — Reference Taxa

Selected plants presented as full botanical accounts, with a whole-plant view and enlarged diagnostic details. Purpose: support identification and comparison between related species.

Plate Group III — Ecosystems

Plants shown within their habitats, including forests, marshes, mountain slopes, deserts and coastal environments. Purpose: connect morphology with ecology without suggesting that habitat alone determines identity.

Plate Group IV — Magical and Anomalous Plants

Unusual specimens accompanied by evidence notes, observation conditions and clear distinctions between confirmed characteristics and unresolved claims. Purpose: document extraordinary observations without turning hypotheses into facts.

Plate Group V — Comparative Plates

Closely related species, look-alike plants, hybrid forms and examples of misleading resemblance. Purpose: teach the reader to identify meaningful differences rather than relying on superficial appearance.

Rules for Preparing a Botanical Plate

Each plate should include a plate number, title, taxonomic identification or provisional name, scale information where relevant, and a short legend explaining the structures shown.

Use consistent lettering for details. If a drawing includes a whole plant and enlarged structures, label the views clearly—for example, A for the whole plant, B for the leaf, C for the flower and D for the fruit.

Whenever possible, show variation rather than a single idealised form. Two leaves from the same species may differ in size or shape; a plate that shows only one perfect specimen may give a misleading impression.

Scientific illustration is most useful when it directs attention towards the features that matter for identification. It should be accurate, selective and legible.

For magical plants, maintain the same standards. If a flower glows only under particular conditions, the caption should state those conditions. If its behaviour has been reported but not independently confirmed, the illustration should not imply that the claim is established.

An artist may depict the extraordinary. A scientific plate must also tell the reader what the evidence supports.

14.8. The Department's Reference Principles

The Department of Botany maintains the following principles for all reference materials produced under the Grand Library's name.

  1. Every claim must have a status. Distinguish direct observation, accepted identification, interpretation, report and speculation.
  2. Every specimen must have a traceable record. Collection numbers, labels, field notes and later annotations must remain linked.
  3. Every identification must be open to examination. Where possible, preserve the material and descriptive evidence needed for another researcher to verify the determination.
  4. Every illustration must have a purpose. A plate should clarify structure, aid comparison, document an observation or communicate a relevant ecological relationship.
  5. Every revision must leave a history. New evidence may alter an identification or classification, but previous determinations should not be erased without a trace.
  6. Every unresolved question must remain distinguishable from a settled conclusion. A catalogue can preserve uncertainty without pretending to have resolved it.

These principles apply equally to familiar plants and to those whose properties appear extraordinary. A rare flower does not require less documentation because it is beautiful, nor does an unusual plant deserve a less rigorous investigation because its behaviour is difficult to explain.

The more consequential a claim, the more important it is to preserve the evidence on which it rests.

A Final Note from Professor Elowen Thorne

When I began my work in taxonomy, I believed that experience would gradually remove uncertainty from my studies. I imagined that the longer I worked, the fewer questions would remain unanswered.

The opposite has proved true.

Each new identification has led me towards relationships I had not previously considered. Each corrected record has revealed another weakness in the catalogue. Every unfamiliar specimen has reminded me that the known flora of the Realms is not the same thing as the entirety of the flora itself.

I no longer consider this a failing.

A library is not diminished by the existence of books it does not yet possess. A catalogue is not rendered useless because some entries require revision. A classification is not worthless because nature occasionally refuses to fit its boundaries.

These systems are valuable precisely because they allow knowledge to accumulate, be compared, corrected and shared.

I have tried throughout this volume to offer the reader a reliable method: observe the plant, describe its structure, examine the evidence, compare it with what is already known, and make every conclusion proportionate to the information available.

The method will not answer every question. It should, however, make it easier to recognise which questions remain unanswered.

There will be plants that have never been collected. There will be specimens whose names have been lost, species whose relationships are disputed and phenomena that we cannot yet explain. Some of these mysteries may be resolved by the next generation of botanists. Others may require methods we have not yet developed.

Our responsibility is not to pretend that the work is finished.

It is to leave the next researcher with better records, clearer descriptions and fewer unexamined assumptions than we inherited ourselves.

If this volume has accomplished that, it has served its purpose.

The Living Index is not a complete inventory of the Known Realms. It is an invitation to continue one.
