The Heart of the Stone Oven

A stone oven has its own rhythm. In the old bakeries, the fire was lit well before the first loaves were ready. The stone needed time to drink in the heat, and no amount of impatience could hurry it.

Once the oven was hot enough, the embers were drawn aside or removed, the hearth was swept clean, and the bread was slipped inside with a long wooden peel. The stored heat baked the loaves even after the flames had gone.

I like to imagine an apprentice asking when the oven would be ready. The old baker would point to the hearth and tell him to watch, learn and wait. There are things a clock cannot teach you.

001 — Traditional Sourdough Starter

Foundation recipe · 7–14 days or longer · Daily attention

Illustration to come

Ingredients

  • Whole wheat flour, 50 g for the first day
  • Drinking water, 50 g for the first day
  • Additional flour and water for daily feedings (50 g each per feeding)

Preparation

  1. Day 1: Mix 50 g whole wheat flour with 50 g water in a clean jar. Cover loosely, leaving room for expansion. Keep at approximately 22–26°C.
  2. Day 2 onward: Retain 50 g starter and discard the remainder. Mix in 50 g flour and 50 g water every 24 hours. Mark the level on the jar.
  3. Continue daily. Early bubbling may be followed by a quiet period; this is normal. If the starter consistently peaks and falls well before the next feeding, feed every 12 hours.
  4. Once it reliably doubles within roughly 4–8 hours after feeding for several consecutive feedings, smells pleasantly acidic and has abundant bubbles, it is ready for bread. This may take longer than two weeks.
  5. For maintenance, feed regularly at room temperature or refrigerate a mature starter and feed about weekly. Before baking, refresh at room temperature until reliably active again.

Care of the Starter

Use clean utensils and potable water. Do not taste raw starter. Discard the entire culture if you see mould, pink or orange discoloration, or a putrid smell. Do not use immature discard in recipes. A mature starter may be maintained with strong white flour or whole wheat flour.

The Baker’s Notes

Give the dough its time. A living starter is not something to hurry; watch how it rises and learn its rhythm.

Flour from Above

In one of the bakeries where I worked, the oven was below street level. Flour deliveries did not have to come down the stairs: small doors at street level opened onto a sloping chute leading down to the bakery.

The sacks of flour slid down the chute to the lower floor, where they could be collected for the day's work. It was a simple arrangement, well suited to a bakery tucked beneath the street.

A Month of Firewood

In those days, a wood-fired bakery depended on regular deliveries of firewood. The wood was brought to the bakery on a cart, ready to fuel the oven and keep the bread coming.

Once, a man who delivered firewood had trouble with his cart. I helped him repair it so that he could carry on with his work. In return, he gave me a whole month of firewood free of charge.

A month of fuel was a generous gift for a baker. Sometimes the kindness exchanged between two working people is worth remembering just as much as the bread itself.
