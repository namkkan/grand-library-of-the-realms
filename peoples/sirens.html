01 · General classification

Neither fish nor human.

The Sirens are sapient marine peoples whose bodies are built for life in water. Though their faces and dexterous hands may appear familiar to surface visitors, their anatomy is unmistakably aquatic: powerful tails, specialised fins, protected gills and skin that must remain moist.

It would be a mistake to describe the Sirens as a single civilisation. Their populations inhabit different seas and depths; their cultures, languages and customs are not determined by their biology alone.

02 · Integument

Skin, scales and living patterns

Most of a Siren's skin is smooth, flexible and covered by a thin protective mucus layer. Scales occur in selected areas rather than over the entire body: commonly along the flanks, exposed forearms and the muscular tail, where they help protect against abrasion without restricting movement.

The face, lips and delicate tissue around the eyes and gill openings remain largely unscaled. Pigmentation varies between individuals and populations: mottling, stripes, iridescence and pale or dark markings may all occur. Scars and irregular scale patterns can distinguish one individual from another.

Prolonged exposure to dry air can damage both skin and gill tissue. The ability to breathe air should never be mistaken for independence from water.

A useful distinctionA smooth face and a scaled tail belong to the same body. Neither is a sign of a different species.
03 · Fins & movement

Built to turn with the current

The elongated trunk leads into a strong tail, which provides propulsion through lateral undulations. The caudal fin supplies thrust, while other fins help with steering, braking and stability. Small stabilising surfaces may occur near the forearms or along the lower trunk, allowing fine work while suspended in water.

Fin shape is not uniform. Broad, flexible fins can favour precise manoeuvres in sheltered coastal habitats; narrower, firmer fins suit sustained travel through open water. Deep-water populations may have compact or specialised fins suited to slow, economical movement.

Sirens possess two arms and dexterous hands, generally with four digits including an opposable thumb and partial webbing. Their hands are capable of handling tools, making records and practising crafts.

Do not confuse beauty with functionA splendid fin may also be a cumbersome one when a strong current rises.
04 · Head & perception

To hear the sea move

The head is recognisably expressive but distinctly marine: a reduced nose with sealable nostrils, protected auditory openings without prominent external ears, and eyes adapted to underwater focus. A transparent protective membrane shields the eyes. Some populations possess cranial crests or sensory filaments rather than human-like hair.

Hearing is sensitive to vibrations carried through water. Chemosensory organs detect dissolved substances, while a lateral sensory system along the flanks registers nearby water disturbances. Touch is particularly refined around the hands, lips and certain fins.

These senses have limits. The lateral system does not grant knowledge of distant events, and the ability to navigate in darkness varies considerably with habitat.

Field question · Can all Sirens see in darkness?

No. Deep-water populations may have exceptionally sensitive eyes, reduced eyes or other sensory specialisations. No single adaptation applies to every Siren.

05 · Dual respiration

Two ways to draw breath

Sirens possess both gills and lungs. Underwater, water passes across protected gill surfaces, allowing the exchange of dissolved gases. In air, the gill openings close against drying and the lungs take over. Both systems are functional, though their relative importance varies with the population.

The gills must remain moist even while the lungs are in use. A Siren may remain out of water for extended periods under suitably humid, supportive conditions, but their tail and considerable body mass make movement on land difficult and potentially exhausting.

Deep-water populations may have particularly flexible thoracic structures and lungs that can remain collapsed under pressure. These fictional adaptations do not make abrupt changes of depth safe.

Surface misconceptionBreathing air is not the same thing as walking on land.
06 · Reproduction

Life before the first tide

Sirens reproduce ovoviviparously. Developing young remain within flexible-shelled eggs retained inside the gestating parent's body. Embryos draw most of their nourishment from yolk reserves, while specialised maternal tissues support gas exchange and waste removal.

Gestation is provisionally estimated at twelve to sixteen months. One or two young are the usual outcome. The eggs hatch shortly before or during birth, which ordinarily takes place in water.

Newborns can make basic swimming movements and use their gills, but require prolonged care and protection. Their lungs are formed at birth; coordinating air breathing and the closure of the gill openings develops with experience.

A note on certaintyGestation times are estimates recorded by the Library, not measurements confirmed for every population.
07 · Growth & longevity

A long life beneath the waves

Sirens grow slowly compared with humans. Early childhood is devoted to swimming coordination and respiratory control; later years bring a lengthening tail, strengthening muscles and the emergence of population-specific physical traits. Physical maturity is generally reached around twenty-five to thirty years of age.

Adult Sirens are usually longer than humans, though much of that length belongs to the tail. Coastal adults are provisionally recorded around 2.1–2.8 m, pelagic adults around 2.6–3.8 m, and deep-water adults around 2.2–3.4 m. These are overlapping tendencies, not strict biological divisions. Adult mass often falls around 100–280 kg, with larger individuals exceeding that range.

A lifespan of 100–160 years is considered common in the Library's preliminary record, while exceptional individuals may live beyond 180. Actual survival varies with health, food availability, disease and environmental hazards. Physical maturity does not dictate the age of social adulthood in every community.

How is length measured?

From the head to the farthest tip of the caudal fin, not as an upright standing height.

On ageNever assume a century-old Siren is old in the same way a century-old human would be.
08 · Ecological variation

One sea, many worlds

The Library distinguishes three broad ecological groupings for field study. They are not formal subspecies, and cultural boundaries do not necessarily follow them.

Sunlit and coastal watersCoastal Sirens

Often agile in reefs, bays and rocky shallows, with comparatively good colour vision and tolerance for changing coastal conditions. Some are well accustomed to alternating between air and water.

Open water and long journeysPelagic Sirens

Often streamlined, muscular and adapted to sustained swimming across broad oceanic distances. Some inhabit dim intermediate waters and rely heavily on orientation and water-borne signals.

Dark and high-pressure environmentsDeep-Water Sirens

Specialised for cold, pressure and limited food. Some display biological bioluminescence, while others rely on highly sensitive nonvisual senses. Their physiology can differ substantially from that of coastal populations.

09 · Physiological limits

What the sea permits

Even an exceptionally adapted body has limits. Sirens need moisture to protect skin and gills; most struggle to support their weight on land. Rapid changes in pressure, temperature or salinity can cause serious harm. Water low in dissolved oxygen may restrict activity, and aquatic infections and parasites remain genuine threats.

10 · Magic & mistaken legends

Not every voice is a spell

Magic is not an innate universal trait of the Sirens. Some individuals develop magical abilities through personal aptitude or study, including disciplines involving currents, perception, sound or light. Others possess no magic at all.

Siren song is not inherently hypnotic. Singing may serve art, communication or ceremony, while a magically gifted singer might produce effects beyond the ordinary. Tales told by surface sailors should not be treated as evidence that every Siren can enchant a listener.

“The first mistake of many surface scholars is to speak of the Sirens as though the sea were a single country.”
