The Kenku

01 · Biological notes

Classification

Kenku include ravens, crows, magpies, jays and other corvids. Their feather colours follow those of natural corvids, while their eyes can be any colour. Size varies between kinds; most have light bodies suited to flight, and differences between sexes are subtle.

02 · Biological notes

Wings, hands and feet

A Kenku's arms are also their wings, not a separate pair of limbs. Each ends in a four-fingered clawed hand. The fingers settle into the wing while flying and unfold for delicate work. Birdlike digitigrade legs, grasping feet and a feathered tail complete their anatomy.

03 · Biological notes

Feathers and the annual moult

Most of the body is covered in smooth, close-fitting feathers. A major moult takes place once a year. Kenku rest, eat more and preen frequently during this period. Some can still fly; others cannot until the new flight feathers are ready. Mature feathers have no sensation when broken, but growing feathers can bleed. Shed feathers may be kept, reused or discarded.

04 · Biological notes

Keeping the plumage in order

Water, dust, natural oils and careful preening all help maintain the feathers. Beaks and claws wear down naturally, though tools and help from companions are common. The base of the wings is particularly sensitive to touch.

05 · Biological notes

The other shape

Each Kenku can become the ordinary corvid corresponding to their own appearance. The transformation is nearly instant, often accompanied by a swirl of feathers. Clothes and small objects change with them. Experience determines how long they can remain transformed. They can speak, imitate sounds or use avian calls in bird form. Young Kenku may discover the ability spontaneously and learn control from family or tutors. Injuries persist between forms, and severe injuries can prevent transformation.

06 · Biological notes

Food and energy

Kenku are warm-blooded omnivores with a digestive system adapted to their larger, flight-capable bodies. Insects, fruit, nuts, eggs, fish, meat, bread, sweets and cooked meals are all possible foods. They often eat small meals throughout the day and need extra food after long flights. With water and rest, they may survive several days without eating, though flying becomes difficult.

07 · Biological notes

Rest and sleep

Soft nests are common, but sleeping habits vary. Kenku often curl up with folded wings and their heads tucked into their feathers. Some sleep only four to six hours, depending on age and activity. Travellers rest in trees or other safe places. Only very experienced flyers can briefly sleep in flight. Fatigue often shows as lowered wings and tail, quietness and a desire for warmth.

08 · Biological notes

Eggs and growing up

Kenku lay eggs, with clutch size varying by kind. Both parents incubate them. Hatchlings emerge without feathers and with their eyes closed. First flights usually occur between one and three years of age; physical maturity generally arrives between twenty and thirty, depending on the kind of Kenku.

09 · Biological notes

Ageing

The usual Kenku lifespan is about 150 to 180 years, although some individuals live longer. Plumage can fade or develop pale feathers; beaks may show wear and fine cracks. Wings may weaken with age, but healthy elders can remain capable flyers. Some methods of magical life extension exist, though they are dangerous.

10 · Biological notes

What feathers can say

A happy Kenku may shake their wings or flick their tail. Anger can raise the neck feathers or bring a stamp of the claws. Affection is shown through touching beaks, resting together and exchanging little gifts. Embarrassment may cause them to hide behind a wing or freeze. Curiosity shows in tilted heads, close inspection and constant questions. They remember faces and sounds exceptionally well, recognise individuals through several cues, have a strong sense of direction and are drawn to shiny objects.
