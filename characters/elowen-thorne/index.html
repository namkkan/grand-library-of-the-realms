Professor Elowen Thorne

“The world becomes no less wondrous when we learn how it works.”

Department of Botany · Taxonomist · Field Naturalist

A respected botanical scholar whose work combines meticulous classification with an enduring commitment to the protection of the living world. Elowen has spent a lifetime learning to read the smallest signs of change in plants—and has never been quite as skilled at reading people.

Visual Notes

Elowen has a weathered face, pale blue eyes and round spectacles that tend to slide down her nose. Her white or silver hair is usually tied back without much care.

She wears practical linen shirts, pocketed waistcoats, sturdy skirts or trousers, worn boots and repaired garments. Her clothes are chosen for fieldwork, not for appearances.

Temperament

Elowen is brilliant, observant, independent and intensely curious. She can spend hours examining a single specimen, comparing its features and recording every meaningful difference. She does not believe that understanding nature removes its mystery; to her, every answer reveals more questions.

She is respected for her publications and for noticing problems other researchers overlook. Yet she can be difficult to approach: she speaks with disarming frankness, interrupts herself when something catches her attention, and often forgets that other people do not share her sense of urgency.

She is not cruel by nature, nor is she secretly waiting to be rescued from solitude. She has built a life she genuinely enjoys, even if that life has left her out of practice with ordinary social rituals.

Origins: A Child Who Preferred the Woods

As a child, Elowen felt more at ease outdoors than in crowded rooms. While other children played together, she might spend hours watching insects, tracking a plant's growth, or returning to the same patch of woodland to see what had changed.

She was not necessarily lonely or friendless. Nature offered a kind of companionship that did not require conversation, performance or meeting anyone's expectations. A plant might appear dead and return weeks later; two nearly identical specimens might behave differently; a flower might bloom only under very particular conditions. Where others saw oddities, Elowen saw questions.

Her curiosity became a habit of keeping records: first drawings, then notebooks, and eventually careful descriptions. Finding something beautiful was never enough. She wanted to know what it was, how it lived and what distinguished it from everything else.

For Elowen, understanding has never diminished wonder. It has only given wonder more room to grow.

A Solitude of Her Own

As an adult, Elowen built her life around the time and concentration that research demanded. She gradually stopped arranging her days around dinners, invitations and social obligations she did not consider necessary.

Her home became an extension of her work: plants on the windowsills, labelled specimens, open books, stacked notebooks and field tools drying by the door. Eventually, the boundary between home and laboratory became difficult to distinguish.

She does not live alone because she hates people. She can respect them, care for them and enjoy a genuinely interesting conversation. She simply does not need constant company. She may value someone deeply and still be relieved when a visit comes to an end.

The Work of Preservation

Conservation gives her work a purpose beyond classification. Elowen has seen plant populations disappear through habitat destruction, irresponsible collecting and the exploitation of rare species. In the Known Realms, magical plants may face additional dangers: their sap is used in potions, their flowers become coveted curiosities, and exaggerated claims about their properties can lead collectors to strip entire habitats.

Her work combines field observation, reliable records and practical methods of protection. Where appropriate, she preserves seeds or maintains living specimens away from their original habitat—but she knows that keeping a plant alive in a greenhouse is not the same as saving the ecosystem it came from.

Contradictions: When Virtues Go Too Far

Rigor becomes control

She cannot tolerate a specimen label being changed without a record of why. She checks assistants' notes and repeats measurements that others have already verified. She believes knowledge must be open to collective correction, yet struggles to trust collective work.

Honesty becomes bluntness

If a researcher presents a conclusion without sufficient evidence, Elowen may point it out in front of the entire department. She does not mean to humiliate anyone; she believes an unsupported claim should not pass unchallenged. She offers plants the patience required to grow, but not always the patience people need to learn.

Dedication becomes obsession

An endangered species can keep her awake for several nights. She forgets meals, postpones household tasks and leaves tea untouched beside her notes. When told she is exhausted, she promises to rest when the work is finished—a moment that rarely arrives on time.

Experience becomes pride

She accepts, in principle, that she can be wrong. In practice, it is difficult for her to admit that a younger or less formally trained researcher has noticed something she missed. When she finally concedes, she may still separate a correct conclusion from a flawed explanation.

Independence becomes emotional distance

When someone she cares about is struggling, she gathers information, prepares a solution and may leave food at their door. Asking how they feel is much harder. She can spend months protecting a plant that will never know who saved it, yet struggle to tell a person that they are not alone.

When the Evidence Proves Her Wrong

A young researcher once came to Elowen with a warning that a population of magical flowers was disappearing. Elowen examined the samples and found an error in the young woman's measurements. She pointed it out with devastating frankness, and the researcher left in embarrassment.

That night, Elowen discovered that the warning had nevertheless been correct. By morning, she had organised an expedition, prepared the equipment and written a note acknowledging the importance of the young woman's observations. She had no elegant apology ready, so she said:

“Your measurements were imprecise. Your concern was justified. We leave at dawn.”

Elowen does not always know how to repair a relationship with words. But when the evidence shows that she must change course, she is capable of changing her actions.

Care, Expressed Indirectly

Elowen rarely expresses affection through spontaneous embraces, compliments or declarations of longing. If asked whether she is worried about someone, she may say that there is no reason to be—even after spending hours waiting for news.

  • She remembers. A passing comment about a plant from someone's childhood may stay with her for years. She might save seeds for that person without explaining why.
  • She prepares. If an assistant returns injured from an expedition, she reprimands them for poor precautions and then checks their equipment herself to prevent it happening again.
  • She struggles to ask for company. She can go months without writing. If she needs help, she drafts what looks like a professional request, even when she has simply missed someone.
  • She finds apologies difficult. Admitting a scientific error is straightforward; saying “I hurt you, and I'm sorry” has no established method in her notebooks.

Elowen chose solitude and remains content with it. Still, she may enjoy someone quietly working in her kitchen while she reads, notice familiar footsteps missing from the corridor, or feel unexpectedly unsettled when a person leaves without saying goodbye. Sharing her life need not mean surrendering it.

“I have kept your specimens separate from the others. I thought you would prefer that.”

Research and Magical Botany

Elowen does not study magic to wield great spells or dominate it. She wants to understand what happens when magic meets plant life. Does a luminous plant produce light through a natural process, a magical one, or both? Does magic change a species' classification, or only one of its traits? Can plants adapt to magical environments across generations? How can one distinguish genuine plant consciousness from an unfamiliar response to stimuli?

For her, “it's magic” is no more satisfying than “it's impossible” when neither statement has been investigated. Every extraordinary claim deserves careful observation, clear records and a willingness to revise the explanation.

Words to Remember

“Preserving a plant is not the same as preserving the conditions that allowed it to live.”
